## Overview

Released on March 9, 2018 via Epic Records, Judas Priest's eighteenth studio album *Firepower* delivered the band's strongest commercial performance since 1990's *Painkiller*. It hit #5 on both the UK and US album charts, earned UK Gold certification, and saw the single "Lightning Strike" nominated for a 2019 Grammy Award for Best Metal Performance. For Scott Travis — the American drummer who joined Priest in 1989 and powered *Painkiller* the following year — *Firepower* marked his thirty-year anniversary milestone with the band and his first studio Priest album since 2008's *Nostradamus*.

The album's production team was a deliberate, unusual pairing. Andy Sneap — producer to the modern metal generation (Accept, Exodus, Testament) and a guitarist himself — handled the contemporary production at his own Old Mill Recording Studios. Alongside him was Tom Allom, the producer behind every classic Priest studio album from *British Steel* (1980) through *Ram It Down* (1988). Allom's return marked his first Priest production credit in thirty years, and the dual-producer chemistry — Sneap's modern clarity matched with Allom's classic-Priest perspective on song dynamics and arrangement — gave *Firepower* a sound that felt both contemporary and unmistakably Priest.

For Scott Travis, the story from *Painkiller* to *Firepower* spans twenty-eight years on the same long-standing rig. Travis has been a long-time endorser of ddrum, Paiste Cymbals, DW Pedals, and Vic Firth Drumsticks since his earliest Priest sessions, and that lineup carried through to *Firepower* unchanged — modern touring and studio refinements aside, the relentless double-bass authority that defined his Priest debut runs on the same gear thirty years later.

The album's release was overshadowed by news that founding guitarist Glenn Tipton had been battling Parkinson's disease, with Andy Sneap stepping in to handle his live duties moving forward. Despite that, *Firepower* arrived as a celebration of the band's longevity and Travis's enduring role as the engine room. Three decades into his tenure, Travis was still delivering the sustained double-bass workouts, the precise snare backbeats, and the controlled-but-explosive feel that made him the right call to replace Dave Holland in 1989.

This article covers every piece of gear Scott Travis used on *Firepower* and what made the Sneap-Allom production capture his sound with both modern clarity and classic Priest weight.

### The ddrum Dominion Series, Three Decades In

By 2018, Scott Travis's main kit for Priest sessions was still the ddrum Dominion Series shell pack — the same brand and platform he used on *Painkiller* in 1990. Travis has been a long-time ddrum endorser throughout his Priest career, and the Dominion Series has remained his go-to platform across every album since.

The bass drum dimensions deepened over the years: *Painkiller* used 22"x16" kicks tuned tight for maximum machine-gun articulation; *Firepower* uses 22"x18" — two inches deeper, which adds low-end body and sustain without sacrificing attack. For an album with more mid-tempo groove material alongside the speed showcases, the deeper kicks deliver the weight the songs demand. Travis still runs two independent bass drums rather than a double pedal, preserving the foot independence that has defined his playing since the 1980s.

The tom configuration is more compact than the *Painkiller* setup — two rack toms (10", 12") and two floor toms (14", 16") instead of the three-rack-tom arrangement of 1990. Modern Travis has consistently favored a tighter tom array, prioritizing fluency across fewer drums rather than a sprawling palette.

Finished in piano black with chrome hardware, the kit reads as a direct visual descendant of the *Painkiller* Dominion Series — same brand, same aesthetic, three decades of refinement under the surface.

### The Modern Crack: ddrum Scott Travis Signature

For *Firepower*, Travis paired the Dominion Series kit with his ddrum Scott Travis Signature 14"x6.5" snare, the same signature model he has used across his Priest career, built to his own specifications for the high-frequency cut he has favored throughout his career.

The result is a snare that cuts through Andy Sneap's dense, layered guitar production while retaining enough body to feel substantial in the mix. Travis tunes it medium-high with tight snare wires — the same tuning philosophy he has used since *Painkiller*, refined for a more modern target sound.

The die-cast hoops continue from the *Painkiller* era as well, adding to the drum's overall projection and giving Travis the rim-shot clarity he uses for accents and cross-stick work across the album. On "Lightning Strike," the Grammy-nominated single, every backbeat needs to lock against Sneap's tight kick-and-guitar production — the Signature snare delivers that anchor with the authority Priest's heaviest material demands.

### Paiste RUDE and 2002: The Travis Arsenal

*Firepower* documents Scott Travis's long-running Paiste endorsement — the same RUDE and 2002 combination that has defined his cymbal voice since *Painkiller*.

The 14" RUDE Hi-Hats are Travis's pulse layer, bright and cutting for maximum definition at speed. They still cut, with enough harmonic complexity underneath to sit well in Andy Sneap's dense, modern production.

The RUDE crash range (16/18/19, mixing in a 2002 for variety) provides Travis the dynamic options he uses across the album. For *Firepower*'s arrangement-dense songs, fast, clear response is essential — the crashes speak immediately, peak hard, and clear out of the way for the next phrase. The three sizes allow Travis to match accent intensity to song dynamics: 16" for tight punctuation, 18" as the primary, 19" for the bigger structural moments.

The 22" RUDE Power Ride is the album's ride workhorse, delivering articulation that holds its position in the stereo field even when guitars stack to the limit — exactly what Priest's mid-tempo material requires.

The 18" RUDE China provides the trashy accent role it has filled since *Painkiller*, useful for tight modern productions that don't want extended cymbal wash.

## The Engine Room at Thirty Years In

*Firepower* arrived in March 2018 as a celebration of Judas Priest's longevity and a vindication of the band's modern-era lineup. The Grammy nomination, the UK Gold certification, the #5 chart positions on both sides of the Atlantic — these were not nostalgia metrics. They were earned by the album's actual quality, and a meaningful share of that quality was Scott Travis's drumming.

For Travis, *Firepower* marked his thirty-year tenure milestone. The kid from Norfolk, Virginia who had cut his teeth with Racer X and replaced Dave Holland in 1989 was now the longest-serving non-founding member of one of metal's most important bands. Eighteen studio Priest albums had passed since *Painkiller* — and yet Travis was still bringing the same combination of speed, power, precision, and musicality that earned him the job in the first place.

The rig from *Painkiller* to *Firepower* tells a story of consistency, not evolution: the same ddrum Dominion Series shell pack, the same ddrum Scott Travis Signature snare, the same Paiste RUDE/2002 cymbal voice, the same DW 9000 Series double pedal. And the philosophy underneath has not changed either. Tight bass-drum tuning for articulation. A signature snare built for cut. Two independent bass drums. Cymbals that project and define. Heel-up technique with natural rebound. Speed built on relaxation, power built on full technique.

For drummers studying *Firepower*, the lessons extend beyond gear. The album shows what a metal drummer's career can look like at thirty years in — not a fading shadow of past glory, but a fully evolved version of the same playing identity. The speed is still there. The precision is still there. The musicality is still there. What is added is the maturity to deploy each of those qualities exactly when the song requires it.

Andy Sneap's modern production gave Travis's playing the contemporary clarity it deserved. Tom Allom's classic-Priest perspective ensured the arrangement choices honored the band's history. Together they delivered an album that was both a present-tense statement and a long-arc payoff for the listener who had followed Travis from *Painkiller* through *Nostradamus* to here.

*Firepower* did not just match *Painkiller*'s chart performance. It demonstrated that the philosophy behind Travis's drumming — and the philosophy behind Judas Priest itself — could still produce essential heavy metal twenty-eight years later. The engine room was still running.

**Q: What drums does Scott Travis use on Firepower?**

His snare was the ddrum Scott Travis Signature 14"x6.5" with die-cast hoops. It's the same long-standing ddrum setup he used on Painkiller (1990); the 22"x18" bass drums are two inches deeper than the 22"x16" kicks he used in 1990, adding low-end weight for Firepower's more mid-tempo material.

The dual-producer dynamic combined Sneap's contemporary engineering and mixing with Allom's classic-Priest arrangement and dynamics perspective.
