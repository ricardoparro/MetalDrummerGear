# Kill 'Em All Drum Setup: Lars Ulrich's 1983 Kit That Launched Thrash Metal

## Overview

Released on July 25, 1983, "Kill 'Em All" was not supposed to sound the way it did. Metallica arrived at Music America Studios in Rochester, New York with roughly $15,000, a stack of riffs, and Lars Ulrich's budget touring kit. What came out was the founding document of thrash metal.

Lars recorded the album on a Camco kit — an affordable, no-frills setup aimed at working drummers who needed something durable, not something impressive. There were no endorsement deals, no signature hardware, no custom modifications. Just a young Danish drummer with raw aggression and a kit he could take on the road.

The cymbal setup was a straightforward Zildjian A series — the same brand Lars had signed with in 1981, two years before Metallica's debut. There was no scrambling for borrowed gear here; it was simply what Lars already owned and trusted.

What makes the Kill 'Em All gear story so compelling is what it represents in Lars Ulrich's evolution. The following year, he upgraded to a professional Tama Artstar II kit for Ride the Lightning — a kit he kept through Master of Puppets and into a full Tama artist relationship by 1988's ...And Justice for All. The arc from Camco to Tama Starclassic spans four decades and one of the most studied careers in metal drumming.

This article covers every piece of gear on that debut session, the recording context that shaped the raw drum sound, and why the limitations of a budget kit turned out to be exactly what thrash metal needed.

### Lars's Studio Kit: The Camco Oaklawn

The Camco Oaklawn was Lars Ulrich's entry-level touring kit in the early 1980s — a no-frills setup built for drummers who needed something reliable on the road rather than something that would impress a gear reviewer. Lars Ulrich's choice of this kit for Kill 'Em All was not artistic; it was simply what he had, a year before his well-documented 1984 switch to Tama.

The kit's modest shells produced a drier, less resonant tone than the kits favored by professional drummers. In a proper studio context this might have been a limitation. At Music America Studios in 1983, with Paul Curcio engineering close and aggressive, it became a feature. The shells' tight, punchy decay contributed directly to the album's raw, in-your-face drum sound.

The 5-piece configuration — 22" bass drum, 12" and 13" rack toms, 16" floor tom — gave Lars a compact setup that suited the fast, riff-driven nature of the material. There was no space for elaborate tom runs; the music demanded driving kick patterns, crashing accents, and relentless forward momentum.

The natural finish was simply what came standard. At this stage in Metallica's career, visual presentation was irrelevant. The kit existed to record an album, not to sell a brand. That functional clarity is part of what makes the Kill 'Em All drum sound feel so honest.

### The Workingman's Camco Snare

Lars's snare on Kill 'Em All was a Camco 14x5.5 chrome-plated steel snare — again, a functional choice rather than a strategic one. Steel snares were standard issue for working drummers in the early 1980s, delivering the bright, cutting crack Lars would carry forward once he moved to Tama's steel-shell snares in 1984.

The 5.5" depth sits between the more common 5" and 6.5" depths, providing a balanced voice: enough body to cut through a recording without the exaggerated depth that makes snares sound boomy. For the Kill 'Em All sessions, the steel shell's crack was captured raw, with minimal processing, resulting in a snare sound that feels physical and present.

Compared to the Tama steel-shell snare Lars would use starting with Ride the Lightning and carried through Master of Puppets, this Camco snare sounds rawer and less refined. The Tama's construction delivers a brighter, more cutting crack; the Camco snare sounds like a drum someone actually hit in a room on a tight budget. That quality — unprocessed physicality — became central to Kill 'Em All's identity.

The tuning, like the rest of the kit, was practical rather than refined. Lars tuned for attack and cut to drive the fast tempos, but without the high-precision approach he would develop over subsequent albums.

### Zildjian A Series: Signed Before the Fame

The cymbal setup on Kill 'Em All was a straightforward Zildjian A series rig — the same brand Lars had signed with in 1981, two years before Metallica's debut. There was no mixed-brand improvisation here; Zildjian A was simply what Lars already played.

The Zildjian A series, with its bright, cutting sound and musical complexity, was well-suited to the fast, hard-hitting demands of thrash. The series was popular among rock and metal drummers for its durability and cutting projection, qualities that remained relevant as Lars pushed the cymbals through demanding studio and live work.

The hi-hats and crashes delivered the fast, articulate attack the material demanded, while the ride and China cymbal rounded out the setup with body and aggressive punctuation — a cohesive single-brand rig from day one.

Lars stuck with Zildjian A exclusively through Ride the Lightning, Master of Puppets, and beyond, making Kill 'Em All the starting point of one of metal drumming's longest-running cymbal relationships rather than a stopgap before a brand switch.

## Year Zero: Why Kill 'Em All Still Matters

Kill 'Em All is the answer to the question of where thrash metal came from. Not the theoretical answer — the specific, documented, gear-and-room answer. A 20-year-old Danish drummer, a budget touring kit, a studio in upstate New York, and four weeks to capture something that had never been recorded before.

The Camco kit was not a great drum kit by professional standards. Its shells lacked the resonance of the premium kits Lars would later play; its hardware was entry-level; its finish was whatever came standard. None of that mattered. What mattered was that Lars Ulrich played it with complete conviction, and Paul Curcio captured the sound close and dry without attempting to polish away the rawness.

The resulting drum sound — punchy, aggressive, slightly rough around the edges — became the template for an entire genre. Countless thrash, death, and extreme metal records have deliberately pursued that Kill 'Em All aesthetic, often with far superior equipment and studios. The Camco kit's sound proved that the most important component in any drum recording is not the kit. It is the drummer's intention.

For anyone tracing Lars Ulrich's gear evolution, Kill 'Em All is the essential starting point. The arc from Camco to Tama Artstar II to the full Tama Starclassic signature setup is one of the most documented progressions in metal drumming history. But that arc begins here, in Rochester, in 1983, with a young drummer who simply hit his drums as hard as he could and trusted that it would be enough.

It was more than enough. It was the birth of thrash metal.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: What drum kit did Lars Ulrich use on Kill 'Em All?**

A: Lars Ulrich recorded Kill 'Em All in 1983 using a Camco 5-piece kit in natural finish — a budget touring model, not a professional or endorsed setup. The configuration included a 22-inch bass drum, 12-inch and 13-inch rack toms, and a 16-inch floor tom. The kit's modest shells produced a dry, punchy tone that was captured raw at Music America Studios in Rochester, New York. This was the kit Lars owned and toured with, the year before his well-documented 1984 switch to Tama during the Ride the Lightning era.

**Q: What cymbals did Lars Ulrich use on Kill 'Em All?**

A: Lars Ulrich used a Zildjian A series cymbal setup on Kill 'Em All — the same brand he had signed with in 1981, two years before the album. The setup included Zildjian A hi-hats, crashes, a ride, and a China cymbal. There was no mixed-brand improvisation; Zildjian A was simply what Lars already played before Metallica had the profile to secure other sponsorships. He stuck with Zildjian A exclusively through Master of Puppets and beyond.

**Q: How was Lars Ulrich's kit on Kill 'Em All different from later Metallica albums?**

A: Kill 'Em All featured a bare-bones Camco budget kit — the entry-level touring model Lars owned before his 1984 switch to Tama. The following year, he upgraded to a professional Tama Artstar II kit with birch shells and a Tama steel-shell snare for Ride the Lightning, a setup he kept through Master of Puppets (1986). By ...And Justice for All (1988), that Artstar II relationship had grown into a formal Tama artist endorsement. The progression from Camco to Tama Artstar II mirrors Metallica's own trajectory from underground indie act to major-label juggernaut — each album's gear reflects exactly where the band stood commercially and artistically at the time of recording.

**Q: Where was Kill 'Em All recorded and who produced it?**

A: Kill 'Em All was recorded at Music America Studios in Rochester, New York in March and April 1983. The album was produced by Paul Curcio on a budget of approximately $15,000. The sessions were fast and lean — the limited budget meant there was no time for extended experimentation, which contributed directly to the raw, energetic character of the drum sound. The album was released on July 25, 1983 through Megaforce Records, the independent label that gave Metallica their first recording contract.

**Q: What bass drum pedal did Lars Ulrich use on Kill 'Em All?**

A: Lars Ulrich used a Ludwig standard bass drum pedal on Kill 'Em All, despite the rest of the kit being Camco. This cross-brand hardware arrangement was typical of drummers who had not yet secured endorsements — each piece was chosen or available independently. The Ludwig pedal's direct chain-drive mechanism delivered the tight, punchy kick attack heard throughout the album on tracks like 'Whiplash' and 'Hit the Lights.' Lars would later standardize on Tama hardware as his partnership with the brand developed.
