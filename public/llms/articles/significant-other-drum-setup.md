## Overview

Released on June 22, 1999, on Flip Records/Interscope, Limp Bizkit's second studio album "Significant Other" became one of the fastest-selling records of the year — debuting at #1 on the Billboard 200, shifting 645,000 copies in its first week, and ultimately achieving 15× Platinum certification in the United States. It was the commercial breakthrough that transformed Limp Bizkit from a rising Jacksonville act into the defining band of late-1990s nu-metal.

At the center of that sonic explosion was John Otto, founding drummer and the rhythmic architect of Limp Bizkit's genre-defining sound. Otto is one of the few drummers in heavy music who genuinely bridges jazz sophistication, hip-hop groove, and metal power in a way that feels completely natural rather than contrived. Modern Drummer described his playing as "grounded in metal, jazz, and hip-hop" — and nowhere is that synthesis more audible than on "Significant Other."

Tracks like "Nookie," "Break Stuff," "Re-Arranged," and "N 2 Gether Now" showcase Otto at peak creative form: syncopated kick patterns borrowed from hip-hop production, snare placements that anticipate Dj Lethal's turntable accents, and the heavy ghost notes and backbeat authority that anchored Wes Borland's aggressive guitar work. The album's drum sound — punchy, groove-driven, and always in service of the song — was a direct result of Otto's playing approach and his gear choices.

For "Significant Other," John Otto played his Orange County Drum and Percussion (OCDP) custom kit — newly adopted from the Pearl setup he'd used since Limp Bizkit's Three Dollar Bill, Y'all$ era — paired with Zildjian cymbals, the endorsement he'd carried from his earliest recordings. Together they delivered the articulate attack and musical warmth his hip-hop-influenced groove approach demanded. This article examines every piece of gear from those sessions and the techniques that produced one of the most commercially successful drum performances in nu-metal history.

### John's 1999 Kit: Orange County Drum and Percussion

John Otto's move to a custom Orange County Drum and Percussion (OCDP) kit around the "Significant Other" era aligned well with his musical priorities: OCDP's maple/birch hybrid shell construction delivers a sound that sits between the warmth of maple and the punchy attack of birch, giving Otto the focused crack on backbeats and the tonal body he needed for the hip-hop-influenced groove patterns that define Limp Bizkit's sound.

OCDP built to Otto's specification rather than off-the-shelf — a step up from the Pearl kit he'd used through Limp Bizkit's Three Dollar Bill, Y'all$ years. The custom shells respond quickly to dynamic variation, allowing Otto to move between subtle ghost note passages and full-power backbeats without the kit losing its character. This dynamic range is critical on "Significant Other," where tracks like "Re-Arranged" require restraint and groove sensitivity, while "Break Stuff" demands explosive, in-the-pocket power.

Otto's single bass drum setup is notable given the heavy double-kick riffs common in nu-metal of the period. Where many contemporaries relied on double kick for aggression, Otto's hip-hop background led him to keep the pulse more groove-oriented, using well-placed kick patterns to lock with DJ Lethal's turntable work and Sam Rivers's bass rather than overwhelm the mix. The 22" bass drum's low-end weight anchored tracks like "Nookie" and "N 2 Gether Now" in exactly the pocket they needed.

The dual floor tom configuration — 14" and 16" — gave Otto the tom range for the dramatic fills on "Break Stuff" and "Counterfeit" without adding excessive rack toms that would crowd his groove-first setup. The custom OCDP hardware — precise, roadworthy, and responsive — allowed Otto to maintain consistent tuning through extended touring and studio sessions.

### The Snare: Cutting Through the Nu-Metal Mix

John Otto's snare on "Significant Other" needed to serve a dual function: crack hard enough to cut through Wes Borland's detuned guitar wall and DJ Lethal's samples, while remaining sensitive enough to articulate the ghost notes and subtle rim accent work that Otto's jazz-influenced groove approach demanded.

A 14" x 6.5" custom OCDP snare provided exactly this balance. Steel-shell construction delivers the immediate, cutting crack that broadcasts authority across a dense nu-metal arrangement — the kind of snare that slaps back at you from a radio speaker or a festival PA without losing definition. At the same time, the 6.5" depth gives the drum enough body that it doesn't sound purely cosmetic; there's tonal presence beneath the crack.

On tracks like "Nookie" — where the verse grooves are comparatively sparse and the snare placement is deliberate — Otto's ghost note control is fully audible. The snare's sensitivity at lower velocities lets those subtle strokes register rather than disappear into the mix. By contrast, on "Break Stuff" and the album's aggressive peaks, the full-force backbeats hit with the authority that 15 million buyers responded to.

### Zildjian Cymbals: The Classic Otto Sound

John Otto's Zildjian cymbal endorsement — established during his earliest recordings with Limp Bizkit — carried through the "Significant Other" era, and the brand's reputation for bright, projecting tone suited a nu-metal mix where guitars, bass, turntables, and vocals compete for mix real estate.

Zildjian's A Series, produced from B20 bronze and treated with a traditional bright finish, is characterized by a bright, cutting tone with a focused core and fast response. This character served Otto's playing approach particularly well: the A Series hi-hats provide the precise, definitive chick sound that hip-hop-influenced groove drumming requires, where every open/close articulation needs to register clearly in the pocket. Sloppy, washy hi-hats would undermine the tight-pocket feel that makes tracks like "Nookie" and "N 2 Gether Now" work.

The crash spread — 16" A Custom and 18" A Series — gave Otto two distinct crash voices without overloading his setup. The lighter, faster A Custom crash provides quick, responsive accent work for the song's many groove-based sections; the fuller 18" A Series crash delivers the sustained crash that marks Limp Bizkit's biggest moments. The 20" A ride's clear bell definition allowed Otto to ride clearly in sections where crash-riding would muddy the groove.

The 18" A China added the aggressive trash accent that nu-metal's most aggressive passages required. On "Break Stuff" and the album's harder-hitting sections, the China's cutting, explosive decay punctuates the music's violence without sustaining over the next beat's groove.

## The Album That Defined Nu-Metal Drumming — and Otto's Place in It

"Significant Other" is not just one of the best-selling albums in nu-metal history — it is one of the best drum performances in the genre. John Otto's jazz-informed groove sensibility, his hip-hop pocket, his ghost note sensitivity, and his genre-fusing technique produced a drum track that served 15 million buyers without ever feeling like a compromise.

The newly adopted OCDP custom kit gave him the tonal balance his dynamic range demanded. The Zildjian cymbals gave him the projection and articulation to cut through one of the most densely arranged records of the era. Together, the gear and the technique produced a sound that defined what nu-metal drumming could be at its most musically sophisticated.

Key takeaways for drummers studying this album:

- **Single kick as a compositional choice**: Otto's groove-first approach with one bass drum demonstrates that restraint creates pocket, not weakness
- **Ghost notes in heavy music**: the subtle snare textures on "Re-Arranged" and "Nookie" give the album depth that pure power drumming would erase
- **Zildjian A Series in dense arrangements**: the A Series' projecting, cutting character was a deliberate choice for a production where cymbals had to compete with turntables and detuned guitars
- **Jazz timing in hip-hop contexts**: micro-rhythmic placement — ahead, behind, on the grid by feel rather than by grid — is what makes the groove breathe

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: What drum kit did John Otto use on Significant Other?**

A: On Limp Bizkit's 'Significant Other' (1999, Flip Records/Interscope), John Otto played his newly adopted Orange County Drum and Percussion (OCDP) custom kit — a maple/birch hybrid-shell kit known for its balanced warmth-to-attack character, replacing the Pearl kit he'd used since Limp Bizkit's Three Dollar Bill, Y'all$ era. The configuration included a single 22" bass drum, 10" and 12" rack toms, and 14" and 16" floor toms. Otto paired the kit with Zildjian A and A Custom series cymbals — his cymbal endorsement since his earliest recordings — known for their bright, cutting, high-projection tone that served Limp Bizkit's dense nu-metal arrangements well. See the full drummer profile: [John Otto at MetalForge](/drummer/john-otto). For the follow-up album's setup: [Chocolate Starfish drum setup](/articles/chocolate-starfish-drum-setup).

**Q: What is Limp Bizkit's best-selling album?**

A: 'Significant Other' (1999) is Limp Bizkit's best-selling US album, certified 15× Platinum — making it the band's highest-certified release. However, 'Chocolate Starfish and the Hot Dog Flavored Water' (2000) holds the record for first-week sales in the band's discography: 1.05 million copies in one week, then a record for fastest-selling album in the US. Both albums debuted at #1 on the Billboard 200. Together they represent the commercial peak of Limp Bizkit and of nu-metal's mainstream moment. See: [Chocolate Starfish drum setup](/articles/chocolate-starfish-drum-setup).

**Q: What makes John Otto's drumming style unique in nu-metal?**

A: John Otto is one of the few nu-metal drummers who genuinely integrates jazz technique into a heavy context rather than simply applying hip-hop aesthetics. Otto studied jazz drumming at the Douglas Anderson School of the Arts in Jacksonville, FL — and that education is audible on 'Significant Other' in his ghost note work, his swing-inflected kick timing, and his fill vocabulary, which phrases over bar lines in a jazz-aware way rather than resolving neatly on beat one. Modern Drummer described his playing as 'grounded in metal, jazz, and hip-hop.' His single-kick approach on an album where double-kick was the nu-metal norm demonstrates his groove-first compositional philosophy. Full technique breakdown: [John Otto at MetalForge](/drummer/john-otto).

**Q: What cymbals did John Otto use on Significant Other?**

A: John Otto used Zildjian A and A Custom series cymbals on 'Significant Other' (1999) — the same endorsement he'd carried since his earliest recordings with Limp Bizkit and continues to this day. Zildjian's A Series is cast from B20 bronze and characterized by bright, cutting, high-projection tone — well-suited for nu-metal's dense arrangement where cymbals must cut through detuned guitars, bass, and turntable samples. The setup included 14" hi-hats, 16" and 18" crashes, a 20" ride, and an 18" China. The A Custom crash's clear bell definition and focused decay served both Otto's groove sections and the album's aggressive moments. See: [John Otto at MetalForge](/drummer/john-otto).

**Q: How did Significant Other change nu-metal?**

A: 'Significant Other' (1999) arrived at the peak of nu-metal's mainstream breakthrough — but Limp Bizkit's approach was rhythmically distinct from contemporaries like Korn or Deftones. John Otto's jazz-hip-hop groove foundation gave the band a commercial radio-readiness that extreme-leaning nu-metal bands lacked. The album debuted at #1 Billboard 200 with 645,000 first-week copies and went 15× Platinum US — numbers that validated hip-hop/metal fusion to the major label world. Its success directly enabled the even bigger 'Chocolate Starfish' campaign in 2000. See: [nu-metal drummers top 10](/top10/nu-metal-drummers).
