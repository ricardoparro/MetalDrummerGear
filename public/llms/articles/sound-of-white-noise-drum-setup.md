## Overview

Released on May 25, 1993, Sound of White Noise was the album that redefined Anthrax. Out went Joey Belladonna's signature high-register thrash vocals; in came John Bush, the gravel-throated former Armored Saint frontman whose mid-range power and bluesy phrasing pulled the band into entirely new sonic territory. Out went the precision-thrash blueprint of Among the Living and Persistence of Time; in came a heavier, slower, groove-metal approach that mirrored the early-90s shift across the entire metal landscape. Through it all, Charlie Benante's gear stayed exactly where it had been since the mid-1980s: Tama drums and Paiste cymbals, the foundation his playing had always been built on.

Produced by Dave Jerden at A&M Studios in Hollywood, the record placed Anthrax in completely different production hands than the band had ever worked with. Jerden — fresh off Alice in Chains' Dirt (1992) and Jane's Addiction's Ritual de lo Habitual (1990) — brought a thick, organic, mid-range-heavy production aesthetic that was the polar opposite of the bright, hyper-articulated thrash production of the late 1980s. Drums on a Jerden record breathe — they sound recorded in a real room, with natural resonance and a wider stereo image than the close-miked, dead-room thrash records Anthrax had made with Eddie Kramer and Mark Dodson.

For Charlie Benante, this was a tectonic shift in approach, not equipment. The drummer who had pioneered sustained double bass in thrash with Among the Living and Persistence of Time was suddenly asked to pull back, slow down, and play with weight rather than speed. Tracks like "Only" — the album's most successful single and a song James Hetfield once called the perfect song — center on a mid-tempo groove that prizes feel over technical fireworks. "Black Lodge," co-written with Angelo Badalamenti for Twin Peaks-era atmospherics, is even slower, demanding restraint and dynamic sensitivity. The double bass is still there, but it's used as a structural device rather than a constant presence.

Sound of White Noise was a commercial triumph — debuting at #7 on the Billboard 200 (Anthrax's highest-ever chart position), certified Gold in the US, and proving that Anthrax could survive the lineup change that many fans had considered impossible. Benante's Tama kit and Paiste cymbals gave Jerden's production the same reliable foundation they had given every Anthrax producer before him. This article breaks down the kit, the cymbals, the hardware, and the technique behind one of Anthrax's most important records — and the album that completed the band's transformation from thrash purists into a groove-metal force capable of surviving the genre's biggest transition.

## Gear Breakdown

- **Drums:** Tama Tama Artstar Custom (Black finish)
- **Snare:** Tama Tama Artstar Custom Brass Snare, 14" x 6.5"
- **Cymbals:** Paiste — Paiste 2002 / RUDE
- **Hardware / Pedals:** Tama HP35 Camco; Tama Titan Hi-Hat Stand; Tama 1st Chair; Pro-Mark Hickory 2B
- **Heads:** Remo Coated Ambassador (batter), Remo Ambassador Snare Side (resonant)
- **Snare tuning:** Lower, fatter tension than Persistence of Time — more body and groove, less crack

### Benante's 1993 Tama Artstar Custom, Streamlined for Groove

For Sound of White Noise, Charlie Benante stayed on the same Tama Artstar Custom birch kit that had carried him through Persistence of Time (1990) — there was no gear overhaul to match the record's groove-metal pivot, just a Jerden-guided change in how the familiar kit was tuned and mic'd. The dual 22" x 16" bass drums remained Charlie's non-negotiable foundation, tuned lower and damped more heavily than on the thrash records to give Jerden's mid-range-heavy production the thick, physical thud that tracks like "Only" and "Burst" needed.

The tom configuration — three rack toms (10", 12", 13") and a single 16" floor tom — was the same streamlined layout Charlie had used since the late 1980s, matching the album's more focused, groove-oriented arrangements. Where thrash had demanded constant fill activity, Sound of White Noise's slower tempos and weightier riffing rewarded the same economical tom vocabulary Charlie had already been playing.

Tama's birch shells continued to deliver the punchy, upper-midrange-forward attack that had always distinguished Anthrax's drum recordings. Jerden's room-heavy miking approach pulled more natural resonance and low-end body out of the same shells than the tighter, closer-miked thrash productions had — proof that the album's tonal shift came from the studio, not a new kit.

### The Same Tama Brass Snare, Tuned for Groove

Charlie Benante's snare on Sound of White Noise was the same Tama Artstar Custom brass-shell snare he had moved to for Persistence of Time (1990) — no new hardware, just a tuning approach adapted to the groove-metal pivot. The brass shell's warmer, rounder fundamental and strong sustain already bridged the gap between a bright, cutting thrash crack and a fuller, more body-forward voice, which made it well suited to Jerden's mid-range-heavy production without needing to be swapped out.

Tuned lower and fatter than on Persistence of Time, the brass snare on Sound of White Noise has a pocket-friendly voice quite different from the high-tuned crack of the earlier thrash records. On tracks like "Only," the snare anchors the mid-tempo groove with weight and authority. On "Black Lodge," it responds dynamically to the song's quieter, more atmospheric passages without losing presence. This versatility — a snare equally at home in mid-tempo grooves and slow, dynamic passages — was essential to the album's broadened sonic range.

Dave Jerden's drum production approach favored close-miked snares blended with overhead and room microphones, capturing both the immediate attack and the natural resonance of the drum in A&M's tracking room. The brass snare responded beautifully to this approach, producing a sound that feels three-dimensional and physical rather than the dry, forward thrash-snare placement of the late 1980s.

### Paiste 2002: The Constant Through Anthrax's Biggest Sound Shift

Sound of White Noise kept Charlie Benante on the same Paiste 2002/RUDE setup he had played throughout the Joey Belladonna era, despite the record's much bigger stylistic pivot. Paiste's 2002 line already offered the tonal range Charlie needed for the album's broader stylistic reach — bright, cutting cymbals for the heavier sections, and the darker RUDE crash/ride for warmer, more musical dynamic passages.

The 14" Sound Edge Hi-Hats anchored Charlie's groove vocabulary on the record, just as they had since the mid-1980s. The wavy-bottom design gives a tight, focused chick sound for closed playing and a full, controlled open-hat tone for the half-open patterns that drive tracks like "Potter's Field" and "Hy Pro Glo" — a slightly drier, more articulate character well suited to the mid-tempo groove playing that defines Sound of White Noise.

The crash configuration spans bright, fast 2002 crashes (16" and 17") for cutting accents and the darker, warmer RUDE 18" Crash/Ride for musical transitions and dynamic moments. This mix reflects the album's range — Charlie could choose between bright, aggressive accents or fuller, more sustained crashes depending on the song's mood and the arrangement's density.

The 22" Heavy Ride remained Charlie's primary ride voice on the record. Its size delivers a defined bell for accent work and a controlled wash for extended ride patterns, with enough mass to sustain through Jerden's dense mid-range production without disappearing. The 18" China provides the trashy, explosive accent voice that Anthrax had always relied on — though, like the double bass, used more strategically on Sound of White Noise than on the band's pure-thrash records.

## The Album That Proved Anthrax Could Evolve

Sound of White Noise is the album where Anthrax stopped being a thrash band and started being something more flexible — a heavy band capable of operating across genres without losing identity. Charlie Benante's playing did all the work of that transformation while his gear stayed constant: the same Tama kit, the same Paiste cymbals, the same Tama HP35 Camco pedals he had used since the mid-1980s. This was not a cosmetic switch; it was a comprehensive rethinking of how he wanted his familiar drums to sound and what kind of music he wanted them to support.

The album's commercial success — debuting at #7 on the Billboard 200 (Anthrax's highest chart position ever), going Gold in the US, and producing two singles in "Only" and "Black Lodge" that received significant radio and MTV rotation — validated the risk. The fan base that worried John Bush would not work showed up in larger numbers than ever before. The critical reception, mixed at the time, has only grown more positive in retrospect: Sound of White Noise is now widely regarded as Anthrax's most underrated album and arguably the band's high-water mark of the 1990s.

For Charlie Benante, Sound of White Noise documents the moment where his thrash-pioneering identity expanded into a more complete drummer's vocabulary. The double bass technique that defined Among the Living is still present, but used selectively. The groove sensibility that had always been embedded in his playing — audible even in thrash contexts like "Caught in a Mosh" — moves to the foreground. The musical intelligence applied to thrash metal arrangements now serves slower, weightier songs that require different forms of restraint and dynamic control.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: What drum kit did Charlie Benante use on Sound of White Noise?**

A: Charlie Benante used the same Tama Artstar Custom birch kit on Sound of White Noise (1993) that he had played on Persistence of Time (1990) — there was no gear switch to go with the album's groove-metal pivot. The setup included dual 22" x 16" bass drums, three rack toms (10", 12", 13"), and a single 16" floor tom with birch shells. The kit was tuned lower and damped more heavily than on the thrash records, reflecting the album's weightier groove-metal aesthetic.

**Q: Did Charlie Benante switch drum brands for Sound of White Noise?**

A: No. Charlie Benante stayed on his Tama kit and Paiste cymbals for Sound of White Noise, the same brands he had endorsed since the mid-1980s. The album's groove-metal pivot and Dave Jerden's mid-range-heavy production aesthetic were achieved through tuning, damping, and room-heavy miking, not a change in equipment. Benante didn't move away from Tama drums, Paiste cymbals, and Vic Firth sticks until decades later, when he added Roland electronics and switched to Tama's Speed Cobra pedals in 2010.

**Q: What cymbals did Charlie Benante use on Sound of White Noise?**

A: Charlie Benante used Paiste cymbals on Sound of White Noise, the same 2002/RUDE setup he had played throughout the Joey Belladonna era. His 1993 configuration included 14" Sound Edge Hi-Hats, 16" and 17" 2002 Crashes, an 18" RUDE Crash/Ride, a 22" 2002 Heavy Ride, and an 18" 2002 China. This mix of bright 2002 cymbals and the darker RUDE crash/ride gave him the tonal range the album's broader stylistic reach required, without any change in endorsement.

**Q: Who produced Sound of White Noise and how did that affect the drum sound?**

A: Sound of White Noise was produced by Dave Jerden — best known for Alice in Chains' Dirt (1992) and Jane's Addiction's Ritual de lo Habitual (1990) — at A&M Studios in Hollywood. Jerden's mid-range-heavy, organic production aesthetic shaped the album's drum sound profoundly: kick drums tuned lower for weight rather than clicky attack, snare embedded in the mix rather than forward and dominant, room ambience preserved aggressively for a three-dimensional sound. This was a complete departure from Eddie Kramer's live but punchy Among the Living production and Mark Dodson's clinical Persistence of Time approach — achieved on the same Tama kit and Paiste cymbals Benante had always used.

**Q: How is Sound of White Noise different from Among the Living drum-wise?**

A: Sound of White Noise represents a fundamental change in Charlie Benante's drum approach compared to Among the Living, but not in his equipment. Both albums feature the same Tama birch kit and Paiste 2002/RUDE cymbals; where Among the Living (1987) ran a thrash-optimized configuration of dual 22" x 16" bass drums, three rack toms, and two floor toms tuned bright and punchy, Sound of White Noise (1993) runs a streamlined single-floor-tom setup tuned lower and damped heavier for groove weight. Stylistically, Charlie pulled back from sustained thrash double bass in favor of groove-anchored playing with weight, restraint, and dynamic sensitivity — a complete rethinking of technique that supported John Bush's arrival as Anthrax's new vocalist and the band's broader genre pivot.
