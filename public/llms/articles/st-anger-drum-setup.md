# Lars Ulrich's Drum Setup on Metallica's St. Anger (2003)

> Complete breakdown of Lars Ulrich's drum setup on Metallica's St. Anger (2003) — including the most controversial snare in metal history: a borrowed Ludwig Vistalite snare with the wires left off, tuned ultra-tight to produce the polarising metallic 'clang' that defined the album.

## Overview

Released on June 5, 2003, on Elektra Records (Vertigo internationally), Metallica's eighth studio album St. Anger debuted at #1 in 30 countries and arrived as one of the most context-dependent records in heavy metal history. The sessions that produced it were documented extensively in the feature documentary Some Kind of Monster — and that documentary, more than any prior Metallica release, framed how listeners heard the album. James Hetfield entered rehab partway through the writing period, founding bassist Jason Newsted had left the band in January 2001, and producer Bob Rock — who had produced every Metallica album since 1991's Black Album — stepped in to play bass on the record itself. Robert Trujillo joined the band only after recording was complete. This was the last Metallica album Bob Rock would produce.

The drum setup at the centre of those sessions belonged to Lars Ulrich, and the snare sound he committed to became the most polarising single percussive choice in modern metal. Lars's kit for St. Anger was his longstanding Tama Starclassic Maple in Green Sparkle finish, assembled at the band's San Francisco facility — HQ, located inside the Presidio. The snare, though, was a one-off: a Ludwig Vistalite acrylic snare that producer Bob Rock had bought to experiment with and lent to Lars during pre-production rehearsals. Lars gravitated to its raw, rehearsal-room character, kept using it through tracking, and — by his own account in later interviews — left the snare wires off for most of the sessions after stumbling onto the sound by accident. Tuned ultra-tight with the wires disengaged, it produced a dry, metallic "clang" — frequently described as a trashcan lid — that runs through every track on the record. It is not a recording mistake, not the product of broken hardware, and not the result of a bad room: it is a deliberate aesthetic decision, made by Lars and Bob Rock, that the band has defended in interviews ever since. The intent was an industrial, raw, almost garage-band character — a deliberate rejection of the polished groove tones the band had cultivated on the Black Album, Load, ReLoad, and Garage Inc.

The Tama Starclassic Maple kit itself was a substantial rig: dual 22"x16" bass drums, a full rack and floor tom configuration, and a Green Sparkle finish. Cymbals came from Lars's longstanding Zildjian endorsement — a Z Custom and A Custom hybrid setup built for cut and aggression. Pedals were Tama Speed Cobra HP910s — Lars's signature pedal era. Sticks were the aluminium-core Ahead Lars Ulrich signatures he has used for decades, and heads were a full Evans pack: EC2 on toms, Heavyweight on the snare batter, EMAD on the kicks.

This article documents the kit, the snare configuration, and the production decisions that produced the St. Anger drum sound — including a direct answer to the question that has been asked about this record for more than twenty years: yes, the snare really does sound like that on purpose.

### Tama Starclassic Maple, Green Sparkle: The Presidio Kit

For the St. Anger sessions, Lars Ulrich stayed with the Tama Starclassic Maple kit he had built his sound around since the Load era, finished in Green Sparkle and assembled at HQ — the Metallica-owned facility located inside the Presidio in San Francisco. Where St. Anger departed from Lars's usual setup was the snare, not the kit.

The configuration was a true double bass setup — two 22" x 16" kick drums rather than a single kick with double pedal. This was a deliberate choice for St. Anger's relentless, often surf-tempo-meets-thrash kick patterns: two independent drums project two independent attacks, with the physical mass of dual shells producing a heavier low-end footprint in the room than a single kick with double pedal can replicate. Tracks like "Frantic," "St. Anger," and "Some Kind of Monster" depend on this kind of sustained dual-kick weight.

The tom configuration paired two rack toms (10" and 12") over the hi-hat side with two floor toms (16"x14" and 16"x16") on the right — a fuller spread than the snare's stripped-down treatment might suggest, and consistent with the Starclassic Maple setup Lars carried across the surrounding album cycles. The maple shell construction provided the warmth and projection the close-miked, dry production approach still needed underneath the record's raw snare character.

### The Most Controversial Snare Sound in Metal History

The snare drum on St. Anger is the single most discussed piece of metal percussion of the 2000s, and the conversation has not stopped in twenty-plus years. The instrument itself wasn't even Lars's own: producer Bob Rock had bought a Ludwig Vistalite acrylic snare — the clear-shelled "Plexi" model — to experiment with, and lent it to Lars during pre-production rehearsals at Metallica's San Francisco clubhouse. Lars gravitated to its raw, rehearsal-room character and, according to Bob Rock, "just would not go back" to other snares once he'd locked onto that sound.

On top of the borrowed drum, Lars has said in later interviews that the record's signature tone came about by accident: he forgot to turn the snare wires on while working out a riff, liked how the disengaged wires sounded against the part, and decided to leave them off for most of the rest of the sessions. Remove the wire interaction and a snare no longer behaves as a snare — it behaves as a small, tight tom-tom. Combine that with an ultra-high batter head tension and the result is exactly what is heard on St. Anger: a metallic, ringing "clang" with a sharp, almost percussive-industrial character — frequently and not unfairly compared by listeners to a trashcan lid or a steel pipe being struck.

Bob Rock has since acknowledged the choice "probably wasn't the best decision for the actual recordings," while defending the experimental spirit behind it — the familiar sound of the clubhouse rehearsals helped keep the band motivated through a genuinely difficult recording period. It is also the choice that polarised the metal audience more than any other single production decision in the band's history. Some fans hear it as a bold, brave commitment to a raw sonic identity. Others consider it the band's worst single creative decision. Both reactions are valid responses to a sound that, once found, the band committed to.

The snare appears in this configuration on every track of the record. There was no "corrected" version mixed for radio singles. What was committed to tape was what shipped.

### Zildjian Z Custom + A Custom: Cut Over Subtlety

Lars's St. Anger-era cymbal setup was built around his longstanding Zildjian endorsement, blending the heavier, brighter Z Custom series with the more musical A Custom line. The Z Custom B20 alloy is computer-lathed for a consistently bright, projecting voice — well-suited to the close-miked, dry, frequency-dense St. Anger mix where cymbal frequencies needed to cut without the help of room ambience.

The 14" A Custom hi-hats were a continuation of the hi-hat size Lars had used for years — the bright, defined articulation he relies on for fast pattern work and accent transitions. The crash array mixed Z Custom and A Custom voices: heavier Z Custom crashes for sharp, aggressive sectional accents matching the album's raw character, with A Custom 18" providing a more musical crash voice for transitions that needed slightly more body.

The Z Custom 20" ride and A Custom 18" China rounded out the array. The Z Custom ride delivers the bright, cutting bell definition needed for the album's aggressive ride passages, while the A Custom China provides the trashy accent voice that punctuates St. Anger's frequent riff transitions and breakdown moments.

## St. Anger: A Drum Sound the Band Stood Behind

St. Anger remains, more than twenty years after release, the most argued-about Metallica record — and the drum sound is at the centre of nearly every argument. The polarisation is not an accident. The configuration that produced it was deliberate, defended, and committed to across every track on the record.

**The Snare-Wires-Off Decision:**
Leaving the snare wires disengaged on a borrowed Ludwig Vistalite snare and tuning the batter head to ultra-high tension produced the metallic "clang" that defines the album. This was not a hardware fault, not a tracking error, and not the result of a damaged kit. What began as an accident became an explicit aesthetic decision driven by the production brief — raw, industrial, garage-band — and held to throughout the entire record.

**The Tama Kit, Unchanged:**
The Tama Starclassic Maple kit assembled at HQ for the sessions, finished in Green Sparkle, continued Lars's longstanding Tama relationship — the same drum brand he had used since the mid-1980s. The dual 22"x16" kick configuration and the Green Sparkle finish sat alongside the record's real departure: the borrowed Ludwig snare and its disengaged wires.

**The Sessions Context:**
The St. Anger sessions are inseparable from the Some Kind of Monster documentary that captured them. James Hetfield's rehab, Jason Newsted's departure, Bob Rock playing bass on the record, Robert Trujillo joining only after tracking was complete — the album is the product of a band in crisis making the record they could make at that moment, in their own facility inside the Presidio, with the deliberate choice to make it sound unlike anything they had released before.

**For Drummers Studying the Record:**
Whether you read St. Anger as a brave artistic statement or a frustrating misstep, the production choices on the record are worth studying as an example of how far drum sound philosophy can be pushed when a band commits fully to an aesthetic. Snare wires off, ultra-tight tuning, close-mic capture, no gated reverb — every one of those is a deliberate inversion of the conventions the band had defined on the Black Album. That kind of commitment to a sonic identity, even a controversial one, is rare in mainstream metal.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Why does St. Anger snare sound different?**

A: The St. Anger snare sounds different because Lars Ulrich left the snare wires disengaged on the bottom head and tuned the batter head extremely tight for most of the recording. With the wires off, the drum no longer produces the buzzing crack that defines a normal snare — it produces a dry, metallic "clang" that listeners frequently compare to a trashcan lid. Lars has said the choice began by accident — he forgot to turn the wires on while working out a riff, liked the result, and kept it for the rest of the sessions — before it became a deliberate aesthetic decision aligned with the album's raw, industrial production brief, not a hardware problem. The sound has been one of the most polarising production choices in modern metal since the album's 2003 release.

**Q: Did Lars use snare wires on St. Anger?**

A: No, not for most of the record. The snare wires were left disengaged for the bulk of the St. Anger recording. The drum used was a Ludwig Vistalite acrylic snare — a "Plexi"-style snare that producer Bob Rock had bought to experiment with and lent to Lars during pre-production rehearsals. Once the wires were off, the batter head was tuned to extremely high tension, producing the metallic "clang" sound heard across most of the album. This was confirmed by Lars Ulrich and Bob Rock in interviews and documented visually in the Some Kind of Monster film. It was not a recording mistake — it was an aesthetic choice the band has consistently defended for more than twenty years.

**Q: What drum kit did Lars Ulrich use on St. Anger?**

A: Lars Ulrich used his longstanding Tama Starclassic Maple kit on St. Anger, finished in Green Sparkle — continuous with the Tama setup he had used through the Black Album, Load, and ReLoad eras, with no change of drum brand. The kit was configured with two 22"x16" bass drums (a true double bass setup, not a single kick with double pedal), two rack toms (10" and 12"), and two floor toms (16"x14" and 16"x16"). What did change for this album was the snare — a borrowed Ludwig Vistalite acrylic snare, not a Tama. Cymbals were a Zildjian Z Custom and A Custom hybrid, pedals were Tama Speed Cobra HP910, sticks were Ahead aluminium-core signatures, and heads were Evans throughout (EMAD on kicks, EC2 on toms, Heavyweight on the snare batter). The kit was assembled at HQ — Metallica's San Francisco facility inside the Presidio — for the album sessions.

**Q: Was the St. Anger snare sound intentional?**

A: Yes. The St. Anger snare sound was an entirely intentional production decision made by Lars Ulrich and producer Bob Rock. The brief for the album was a raw, industrial, garage-band drum sound that rejected the polished, room-mic-heavy approach of the Black Album, Load, and ReLoad. Disengaging the snare wires and tuning the batter head ultra-tight was the most direct way to deliver that aesthetic. The decision is documented extensively in the Some Kind of Monster film, has been defended in numerous interviews by both Lars and Bob Rock, and was committed to across the entire record — there is no "corrected" version of any track. Listener reception has been deeply polarised since release, but the band has never described the sound as a mistake; it was the sound they meant to make.
