## Overview

Released in 1998 on Century Media Records, Cryptopsy's third studio album "Whisper Supremacy" occupies a critical position in the band's evolution — and in technical death metal history. Recorded two years after the ground-breaking [None So Vile](/articles/none-so-vile-drum-setup) (1996), it arrived two years before the compositional peak of [And Then You'll Beg](/articles/and-then-youll-beg-drum-setup) (2000), serving as the connective tissue between those two landmarks.

For Flo Mounier, "Whisper Supremacy" marked a moment of deliberate refinement. Where "None So Vile" had been a raw assault — ferocious, barely contained, recorded with a ferocity that bordered on chaos — this album showed a drummer settling into his power. The blast beats were still operating at the extreme edge of human capability. The fills were still melodically sophisticated in ways that confounded listeners expecting pure brutality. But the control was more evident, the production more precise, the compositional structure more calculated.

Producer Pierre Rémillard returned to Studio Victor in Montreal — the same setting as "None So Vile" — bringing the same close-mic philosophy and demand for individual stroke clarity. Gear-wise, "Whisper Supremacy" was a continuation rather than a reinvention: Flo recorded on the same Pearl MX Series kit he'd used on "None So Vile," with the Pearl Masters BRX upgrade still two years away and reserved for [And Then You'll Beg](/articles/and-then-youll-beg-drum-setup). No cymbal or pedal brand change is documented for this era of his career either, so this article focuses on what's verified: the kit, the recording approach, and the performance itself.

Lord Worm, still behind the microphone for his final album before the Mike DiSalvo era, pushed the band's extremity with a lyrical and vocal approach that demanded Flo's full technical arsenal. The result is an album that holds a unique position in Cryptopsy's discography: the most complete execution of their original formula before it was deliberately expanded into new territory.

This article breaks down the gear configuration behind one of technical death metal's most important transitional recordings.

### Flo's 1998 Arsenal: Pearl MX Series

"Whisper Supremacy" finds Flo Mounier recording on the same Pearl MX Series kit he used on "None So Vile" two years earlier. The Pearl Masters BRX upgrade — Pearl's flagship professional line — wouldn't arrive until [And Then You'll Beg](/articles/and-then-youll-beg-drum-setup) in 2000, so 1998 still finds Flo on the mid-range MX Series that had served him since Cryptopsy's earliest days.

The 22-inch bass drum configuration carried over unchanged from "None So Vile" — the 26-inch experiment that defined [And Then You'll Beg](/articles/and-then-youll-beg-drum-setup) was still two years away. At 22 x 18 inches, the drum provided the attack speed essential for blast beat clarity. Flo tuned it tight, prioritizing click and attack over sustained low-frequency resonance.

The compact rack tom configuration (10" and 12") also carried forward from the NSV era. Flo's melodic approach to tom work — treating them as pitched accent instruments rather than rhythm anchors — continued to be well-served by the faster response of smaller shells.

The floor toms at 14" and 16" were deployed strategically, as on "None So Vile," for compositional transition moments and melodic fill sequences. With the same kit carried over from 1996, any difference in the recorded drum sound on "Whisper Supremacy" comes down to Flo's increased command of the material and Rémillard's production refinements, not a change in equipment.

### The Blast Beat Core: Pearl Steel Snare

Flo's snare configuration for "Whisper Supremacy" continued the approach that had served "None So Vile" — a Pearl steel snare at 14" x 5.5", tuned high for maximum attack and minimum sustain. The steel shell's natural brightness cut through Cryptopsy's dense guitar frequencies with the same authority it had provided in 1996.

The choice to maintain the steel snare through this transitional album reflected Flo's understanding of what blast beat drumming required. At the sustained tempos "Whisper Supremacy" demanded, each snare stroke needed to speak clearly as an individual event before the next arrived. A warmer wood shell — even one with more tonal character — would have introduced sustain that blurred strokes together at these speeds.

Flo tuned the snare to a very high tension, maintaining the choked, cutting character that blast beat articulation required. The snare wires were set tight, reducing sustain and increasing sensitivity across the full head surface. Producer Pierre Rémillard's close-mic approach captured the steel shell's attack with precision, placing the snare front and centre in the mix where the demanding performance required it to be audible.

### Cymbal Setup: Not Independently Verified

Unlike the drum kit, Flo Mounier's cymbal setup for "Whisper Supremacy" isn't independently documented. His confirmed endorsement history shows no cymbal-brand partnership before 2012, when he adopted Sabian's AAX/HHX series alongside his move to Tama — so any specific cymbal brand or model attributed to Cryptopsy's 1996-2000 era, including this album, should be treated as unconfirmed rather than verified fact.

Given that the kit and overall playing approach carried over unchanged from "None So Vile," it's plausible Flo's cymbal setup also stayed broadly consistent across the two albums. But MetalForge doesn't have a verified source confirming the specific cymbal brand or models Flo used on "Whisper Supremacy," so we're leaving that detail out rather than guessing.

## The Bridge That Held the Arc Together

"Whisper Supremacy" has always existed in the shadow of its neighbours. "None So Vile" (1996) was the landmark — the album that redefined technical death metal's upper limits and influenced every band that followed. "And Then You'll Beg" (2000) was the compositional peak — the album where Flo Mounier proved that extreme metal could be executed in odd time signatures at blast beat tempos without losing structural coherence. Between them, "Whisper Supremacy" served its function: it bridged the gap, refined the approach, and set up the 2000 album's creative leap.

Gear-wise, the album's significance is in what didn't change: Flo recorded on the same Pearl MX Series kit as None So Vile, with the Masters BRX upgrade still two years away. The refinement audible on "Whisper Supremacy" came from two years of touring and performing the None So Vile material, not from new equipment.

For drummers studying Flo Mounier's development:

**Refinement is a career stage**: The controlled intensity of Whisper Supremacy is as significant as the raw extremity of None So Vile — they represent different points on the same development arc, achieved on the same gear
**Transitional albums have their own value**: Not every record needs to be a landmark; some records exist to consolidate and prepare for what comes next
**Consistency over chaos**: The more controlled blast beats of Whisper Supremacy versus None So Vile demonstrate that technical development isn't always visible in tempo — sometimes it's in the steadiness of what's already fast
**Mastery doesn't require new gear**: Two years of touring the same kit gave Flo the control that defines this album, well before the Masters BRX arrived for And Then You'll Beg

The bridge doesn't get the credit the landmarks do. But without it, the journey doesn't complete.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: What drum kit did Flo Mounier use on Whisper Supremacy?**

A: On Cryptopsy's Whisper Supremacy (1998), Flo Mounier played the same Pearl MX Series kit he had used on None So Vile (1996). The Pearl Masters BRX upgrade — Pearl's professional flagship line — didn't arrive until And Then You'll Beg (2000). The configuration included a 22-inch bass drum, 10-inch and 12-inch rack toms, and 14-inch and 16-inch floor toms, all carried over unchanged from the None So Vile era.

**Q: Where was Whisper Supremacy recorded and who produced it?**

A: Whisper Supremacy (1998) was recorded at Studio Victor in Montreal, Quebec, produced by Pierre Rémillard — the same producer and studio as None So Vile (1996). Rémillard's close-mic philosophy, which had produced None So Vile's landmark drum clarity, was applied again on the same Pearl MX Series kit. The return to the same producer, studio, and gear provided consistency across the two albums while allowing Flo's increased mastery of the material to produce a more controlled and defined drum sound than the 1996 recording.

**Q: How does Whisper Supremacy fit in Flo Mounier's discography?**

A: Whisper Supremacy (1998) occupies the critical transitional position between None So Vile (1996) — the landmark extreme death metal performance — and And Then You'll Beg (2000) — Flo Mounier's compositional peak. Gear-wise, the album is a continuation rather than a transition: Flo recorded on the same Pearl MX Series kit as None So Vile, with the Masters BRX upgrade still two years away. Musically, it represents the refinement of None So Vile's raw intensity toward the deliberate compositional complexity of the 2000 album. It is also Lord Worm's last Cryptopsy album until his return on Once Was Not (2005).
