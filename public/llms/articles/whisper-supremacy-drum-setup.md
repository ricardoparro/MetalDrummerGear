## Overview

Released in 1998 on Century Media Records, Cryptopsy's third studio album "Whisper Supremacy" occupies a critical position in the band's evolution — and in technical death metal history. Recorded two years after the ground-breaking [None So Vile](/articles/none-so-vile-drum-setup) (1996), it arrived two years before the compositional peak of [And Then You'll Beg](/articles/and-then-youll-beg-drum-setup) (2000), serving as the connective tissue between those two landmarks.

For Flo Mounier, "Whisper Supremacy" marked a moment of deliberate refinement. Where "None So Vile" had been a raw assault — ferocious, barely contained, recorded with a ferocity that bordered on chaos — this album showed a drummer settling into his power. The blast beats were still operating at the extreme edge of human capability. The fills were still melodically sophisticated in ways that confounded listeners expecting pure brutality. But the control was more evident, the production more precise, the compositional structure more calculated.

Producer Pierre Rémillard returned to Studio Victor in Montreal — the same setting as "None So Vile" — bringing the same close-mic philosophy and demand for individual stroke clarity. The core of Flo's rig carried over unchanged from 1996: the same Pearl MX Series kit that had defined None So Vile's sound. The upgrade to Pearl's flagship Masters BRX line was still two years away, arriving with the 26-inch bass drum of [And Then You'll Beg](/articles/and-then-youll-beg-drum-setup) (2000). The specific cymbal and pedal hardware Flo ran for this session isn't documented in verified endorsement records, so this article sticks to what's confirmed rather than assuming a gear change the timeline doesn't support.

Lord Worm, still behind the microphone for his final album before the Mike DiSalvo era, pushed the band's extremity with a lyrical and vocal approach that demanded Flo's full technical arsenal. The result is an album that holds a unique position in Cryptopsy's discography: the most complete execution of their original formula before it was deliberately expanded into new territory.

This article breaks down the gear configuration behind one of technical death metal's most important transitional recordings.

### Flo's 1998 Arsenal: Pearl MX Series (Continued from None So Vile)

For "Whisper Supremacy," Flo Mounier stayed on the same Pearl MX Series kit that had defined "None So Vile" two years earlier. The upgrade to Pearl's flagship Masters BRX line — the kit most associated with his later work — was still two years away, arriving with 2000's [And Then You'll Beg](/articles/and-then-youll-beg-drum-setup). At the tempos Flo demanded, the MX's mid-range maple shells continued to deliver the clarity and response that had made the kit work on the debut.

The 22-inch bass drum configuration remained unchanged from "None So Vile." At 22 x 18 inches, the drum provided the attack speed essential for blast beat clarity without the slower settling time of a larger shell. Flo tuned it tight, prioritizing click and attack over sustained low-frequency resonance — the same approach he'd used in 1996.

The compact rack tom configuration (10" and 12") carried forward unchanged from the NSV era. Flo's melodic approach to tom work — treating them as pitched accent instruments rather than rhythm anchors — was well-served by the faster response of smaller shells.

The floor toms at 14" and 16" were deployed strategically, as on the previous Cryptopsy recording, for compositional transition moments and melodic fill sequences. What changed between the two albums wasn't the gear — it was two more years of touring and internalization showing up as tighter, more controlled execution of the same instrument.

### The Blast Beat Core: Pearl Steel Snare

Flo's snare configuration for "Whisper Supremacy" continued the approach that had served "None So Vile" — a Pearl steel snare at 14" x 5.5", tuned high for maximum attack and minimum sustain. The steel shell's natural brightness cut through Cryptopsy's dense guitar frequencies with the same authority it had provided in 1996.

The choice to maintain the steel snare through this transitional album reflected Flo's understanding of what blast beat drumming required. At the sustained tempos "Whisper Supremacy" demanded, each snare stroke needed to speak clearly as an individual event before the next arrived. A warmer wood shell — even one with more tonal character — would have introduced sustain that blurred strokes together at these speeds.

Flo tuned the snare to a very high tension, maintaining the choked, cutting character that blast beat articulation required. The snare wires were set tight, reducing sustain and increasing sensitivity across the full head surface. Producer Pierre Rémillard's close-mic approach captured the steel shell's attack with precision, placing the snare front and centre in the mix where the demanding performance required it to be audible.

## The Bridge That Held the Arc Together

"Whisper Supremacy" has always existed in the shadow of its neighbours. "None So Vile" (1996) was the landmark — the album that redefined technical death metal's upper limits and influenced every band that followed. "And Then You'll Beg" (2000) was the compositional peak — the album where Flo Mounier proved that extreme metal could be executed in odd time signatures at blast beat tempos without losing structural coherence. Between them, "Whisper Supremacy" served its function: it bridged the gap and refined the approach that would carry into the 2000 album.

For gear historians, the album's significance is continuity, not transition. Flo recorded "Whisper Supremacy" on the same Pearl MX Series kit that had defined "None So Vile," two years before the Masters BRX upgrade arrived for "And Then You'll Beg." The specific cymbal and pedal brands he ran for this session aren't documented in verified sources, so this page doesn't guess at them.

For drummers studying Flo Mounier's development:

**Familiar tools, refined execution**: Whisper Supremacy shows that technical growth doesn't always require new gear — the same Pearl MX kit that recorded None So Vile also captured a more controlled performance two years later
**Refinement is a career stage**: The controlled intensity of Whisper Supremacy is as significant as the raw extremity of None So Vile — they represent different points on the same development arc
**Transitional albums have their own value**: Not every record needs to be a landmark; some records exist to consolidate and prepare for what comes next
**Consistency over chaos**: The more controlled blast beats of Whisper Supremacy versus None So Vile demonstrate that technical development isn't always visible in tempo — sometimes it's in the steadiness of what's already fast

"Whisper Supremacy" gave Flo Mounier two more years on the same Pearl MX Series kit to refine his technique and internalize the material before Cryptopsy's gear upgrade — and compositional leap — on "And Then You'll Beg." Without that refinement period, the 2000 album's odd-time blast beats and compositional ambition might not have been achievable.

The bridge doesn't get the credit the landmarks do. But without it, the journey doesn't complete.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: What drum kit did Flo Mounier use on Whisper Supremacy?**

A: On Cryptopsy's Whisper Supremacy (1998), Flo Mounier played the same Pearl MX Series kit he had used on None So Vile (1996) — no gear change for this session. The configuration included a 22-inch bass drum, 10-inch and 12-inch rack toms, and 14-inch and 16-inch floor toms, all unchanged from the 1996 recording. The upgrade to Pearl's flagship Masters BRX line, with its 26-inch bass drum, didn't arrive until And Then You'll Beg (2000).

This page won't assign a brand to the 1998 session without a verified source.

**Q: What pedals did Flo Mounier use on Whisper Supremacy?**

**Q: Where was Whisper Supremacy recorded and who produced it?**

A: Whisper Supremacy (1998) was recorded at Studio Victor in Montreal, Quebec, produced by Pierre Rémillard — the same producer and studio as None So Vile (1996). Rémillard's close-mic philosophy, which had produced None So Vile's landmark drum clarity, was applied again on the same Pearl MX Series kit, with refinements driven by two years of additional studio experience rather than a gear change. The return to the same producer, studio, and kit provided consistency across the two albums while Flo's increased mastery of the material produced a more controlled and defined drum sound than the 1996 recording.

**Q: How does Whisper Supremacy fit in Flo Mounier's discography?**

A: Whisper Supremacy (1998) occupies the critical transitional position between None So Vile (1996) — the landmark extreme death metal performance — and And Then You'll Beg (2000) — Flo Mounier's compositional peak. Gear-wise, it's a continuation chapter: Flo stayed on the same Pearl MX Series kit through both the 1996 and 1998 recordings, with the Masters BRX upgrade not arriving until the 2000 album. Musically, it represents the refinement of None So Vile's raw intensity toward the deliberate compositional complexity of the 2000 album. It is also Lord Worm's last Cryptopsy album until his return on Once Was Not (2005).
