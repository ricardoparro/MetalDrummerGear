## Hansha — Polyrhythmic Metalcore Architecture

**Song:** Hansha | **Album:** Hikari (2017) | **BPM:** ~165 BPM | **Technique:** signature pattern | **Difficulty:** expert

"Hansha" from Oceans Ate Alaska's Hikari (2017) is the track that brought Chris Turner's technically sophisticated drumming to its widest audience. Hikari was the album where Oceans Ate Alaska most deliberately blended progressive metalcore with Japanese musical influences, and Turner's drumming underpins that ambition with a performance that is technically exacting without losing its drive or groove. "Hansha" operates around 165 BPM and features one of Turner's most identifiable techniques: polyrhythmic superimposition over a driving metalcore pulse. Rather than playing straightforward metalcore patterns against the riff, Turner layers three-against-four or five-against-four subdivisions over the backbeat, creating a sensation of rhythmic multiplicity — as if the kit is playing in two different times simultaneously — while the overall feel remains locked and purposeful. What makes Turner's approach immediately recognisable is that these polyrhythms are never abstract or academic. They emerge from the musical context of the track — the kick patterns beneath the chorus groove specifically emphasise the same polyrhythmic contour as the melodic content above, creating a compositional unity between drums and arrangement. Turner has cited Matt Halpern and Matt Garstka as his primary influences, and both of these rhythmic sensibilities are audible in "Hansha": Halpern's locked groove foundation and Garstka's willingness to superimpose independent rhythmic layers without destabilising the pulse. The blast sections in "Hansha" are typical of Turner's approach to that technique in metalcore: fast and controlled, but deployed as punctuation rather than sustained texture, entering and exiting with a precision that gives the blasts compositional weight rather than the generic impact blast beats can carry in less considered arrangements. The hand-foot independence required to sustain the polyrhythmic kick layer while playing a compositionally independent hand pattern above it is the central technical challenge of this track. For drummers, this is the ideal entry point into Turner's vocabulary: it demands polyrhythm independence, controlled blast placement, and the discipline to keep complex rhythmic layers serving the song rather than displaying themselves.

### How to Play

- Superimpose three-against-four or five-against-four kick patterns over the backbeat without disrupting the underlying pulse
- Maintain the groove foundation at all times — the polyrhythms should add rhythmic depth, not undermine the metalcore drive
- Deploy blast beats as compositional punctuation, entering and exiting precisely at phrase boundaries
- Align the kick polyrhythm contour with the melodic content of the track for compositional unity between drums and arrangement
- Practise each rhythmic layer — straight backbeat, kick superimposition, blast entries — in isolation before combining them at tempo

### Key Elements

- Isolate the kick polyrhythm pattern before adding the hand pattern — the independence between hands and feet is the core challenge
- Practise at 100 BPM with a subdivided click until the polyrhythm is automatic, then raise tempo in 10 BPM increments
- Record yourself and listen back: the superimposed rhythm should remain audible as a distinct layer, not collapse into irregular kick noise
- Use a metronome set to the polyrhythm's grouping note value to internalise the independent layer before combining with the hand pattern

## Escapist — Technical Double-Bass Drive

**Song:** Escapist | **Album:** Hikari (2017) | **BPM:** ~170 BPM | **Technique:** double bass | **Difficulty:** advanced

"Escapist" from Oceans Ate Alaska's Hikari (2017) demonstrates Chris Turner's command of double bass in a progressive metalcore context. Where many metalcore drummers use double bass as a straightforward 16th-note stream beneath riffs, Turner's double bass on "Escapist" is purposefully composed: the kick patterns shift between straight 16th-note runs, syncopated off-beat accents, and brief triplet interpolations that create rhythmic interest within what is fundamentally a driving metalcore groove. At 170 BPM, the double bass demands real foot technique — the Speed Cobra's sensitivity and Turner's Moeller-influenced foot approach allow him to maintain note clarity at this tempo while the hands handle independent pattern work above. The defining characteristic of Turner's double bass technique, audible throughout "Escapist", is evenness of velocity: both feet produce identical note weight regardless of which foot is leading. Many drummers develop a dominant foot that is louder and more precisely timed than the weaker foot — Turner has clearly invested in independence training that equalises both feet's contribution. This is particularly audible in the transitional fills where the kick pattern breaks from the straight run into the syncopated figure, and both feet need to land with equal precision to make the accent pattern clear. The hand pattern above the double bass on "Escapist" is characteristically Turner: cross-stick hits on the snare that shift position within the beat, ride cymbal accents that reinforce the melodic phrase structure, and hi-hat splashes that add texture at phrase transitions. None of these elements are decorative — they are compositional, each placed to serve the song's momentum. For drummers developing double bass technique for progressive metalcore, "Escapist" is an ideal study piece: the kick work is technically demanding without being extreme, the velocity evenness requirement is immediately audible on recording, and the hand-independence demands are achievable with focused practice.

### How to Play

- Develop velocity evenness between both feet so both produce identical note weight at 170 BPM — dominance in one foot is immediately audible
- Shift between straight 16th-note runs, syncopated accents, and triplet interpolations with no drop in foot precision
- Maintain the compositional hand pattern above the double bass — cross-stick, ride accents, and hi-hat splashes are placed to serve the song
- Lead with the weaker foot in practice to equalise both feet's contribution before combining them at tempo
- Practise kick transitions — 16th to syncopated to triplet — at slow tempo until automatic before raising the BPM

### Key Elements

- Practise the kick pattern with just the feet before adding hands — confirm velocity evenness between both feet before combining
- Use a practice pad under the hi-hat foot to develop the left foot's independent note production at this tempo
- Record the double bass pattern isolated and compare both feet's volume and clarity — any disparity is immediately obvious
- Build the hand pattern separately to automaticity before combining with the foot work at song tempo

## Metamorph — Mathcore Odd-Time Precision

**Song:** Metamorph | **Album:** Disparity (2022) | **BPM:** ~155 BPM | **Technique:** odd time | **Difficulty:** expert

"Metamorph" from Oceans Ate Alaska's Disparity (2022) represents the most developed expression of Chris Turner's approach to odd-time signatures in metalcore. Disparity was the album that demonstrated how far Oceans Ate Alaska had moved from their early metalcore framework — the rhythmic language on "Metamorph" in particular draws on mathcore and progressive metal vocabularies while retaining the intensity and physicality of their metalcore roots. Turner navigates a series of time-signature changes — moving between 4/4, 7/8, and 5/4 passages — with a smoothness that makes the shifts feel compositionally inevitable rather than technically disruptive. This is the advanced application of the skill that "Hansha" begins to develop: where "Hansha" superimposes independent rhythmic layers over a stable pulse, "Metamorph" changes the pulse itself, requiring Turner to reset his internal metric feel at each transition. The key to Turner's execution on "Metamorph" is that he treats each time signature as a groove feel rather than a counting exercise. The 7/8 sections do not sound metronomically calculated — they swing with a forward momentum that is only possible when the drummer has internalised the feel rather than counting each beat. Similarly, the transitions from 5/4 back to 4/4 are executed at the phrase boundary with enough rhythmic confidence that the listener feels pulled forward through the transition rather than jarred by it. Turner's blast work on "Metamorph" appears within the odd-time context: blasting over a 7/8 riff is a specific and demanding challenge, because the truncated bar disrupts the blast's natural phrase symmetry, requiring the drummer to reset the phrasing one beat earlier than the body expects. Turner handles this without audible hesitation — the blast entries and exits in odd time are as precisely placed as they would be in 4/4. For drummers, "Metamorph" is the definitive study piece for developing odd-time metalcore drumming: it demands internalised feel for each time signature, compositional confidence in the transitions, and the advanced blast technique needed to operate outside the standard four-bar framework.

### How to Play

- Internalise each time signature as a groove feel rather than a counting exercise — the transitions must feel inevitable, not calculated
- Execute time-signature transitions at phrase boundaries with rhythmic confidence so the listener is pulled forward rather than disrupted
- Deploy blast beats within odd-time bars by resetting phrasing to the bar length rather than expecting standard four-bar symmetry
- Practise each time signature independently until it feels natural as a groove, then work the specific transition points between them
- Tap the vocal melody while playing the drum part to anchor each time signature to the song's melodic context

### Key Elements

- Practise 7/8 and 5/4 grooves independently with a metronome until both feel like natural grooves, not counting exercises
- Work the transition from 4/4 to 7/8 as a repeating loop before combining it with other time-signature changes
- Record the odd-time blast passages and verify that the blast phrasing resets correctly at the bar boundary — it should not overshoot into the next bar
- Tap the vocal line on your knee while playing the drum part to develop the multi-tasking between melodic phrase and rhythmic grid
