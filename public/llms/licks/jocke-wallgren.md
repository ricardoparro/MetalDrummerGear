## Twilight of the Thunder God Double Bass Run

**Song:** Twilight of the Thunder God | **Album:** Twilight of the Thunder God (2008) — live staple of Wallgren's tenure | **BPM:** ~162 BPM | **Technique:** double bass | **Difficulty:** advanced

"Twilight of the Thunder God" from Amon Amarth's 2008 album of the same name is the band's signature live anthem and the track most associated with their global profile — and Jocke Wallgren, who has served as Amon Amarth's drummer since joining in 2016, has made its thunderous double bass passages one of the centrepieces of his live performance legacy. While the studio recording was made by predecessor Fredrik Andersson, Wallgren has performed the song thousands of times across arena and festival stages worldwide, and his live interpretations are documented across extensive concert footage and audio recordings that demonstrate his powerful, groove-anchored approach to the song's double bass demands. The track operates at approximately 162 BPM and features extended double bass runs that drive the song's epic, cinematic momentum, particularly in the main verse and chorus transitions where the kick pattern shifts from a supporting role to a propulsive, forward-driving force that carries the entire rhythmic weight of the arrangement. Wallgren's double bass technique in this track is characterised by the groove-orientation he brings to speed: rather than treating the double bass passages as pure technical displays, he maintains an underlying rhythmic pulse in the patterns that keeps them anchored in the song's feel. His right and left foot alternation is even and authoritative, with consistent attack weight across both pedals — a crucial factor at 162 BPM, where any dynamic imbalance between feet creates an uneven, stuttering texture that undermines the song's anthemic power. The ride cymbal during double bass passages carries a driving quarter-note or eighth-note pattern that provides rhythmic clarity above the kick density, while the snare accent on beats two and four maintains the backbeat anchor that prevents the double bass from consuming the groove entirely. The transitions between the groove-oriented verse sections and the full-drive double bass chorus passages require Wallgren to shift weight from upper-limb-led playing to leg-led playing instantly and without disruption — a physical skill that demands the independence to run double bass and hand patterns simultaneously without either compromising the other. Live, Wallgren sustains this intensity across full-length festival sets where "Twilight of the Thunder God" often serves as the set-closing peak moment, requiring both the physical endurance to execute the double bass accurately after an hour of high-intensity performance and the mental composure to keep the pattern locked during the arena's heightened emotional atmosphere. For drummers, "Twilight of the Thunder God" as performed by Wallgren is the essential melodic death metal double bass study: it develops even alternation at 160+ BPM, the groove-orientation that distinguishes musical double bass from technical exercise, and the independence to maintain clear ride and snare work above a continuous double bass foundation.

### How to Play

- Alternate both kick pedals evenly at ~162 BPM with identical attack weight on each foot — imbalance between feet creates an uneven texture that undermines the anthemic groove
- Maintain a driving ride cymbal quarter-note or eighth-note pattern above the double bass to provide rhythmic clarity over the kick density
- Keep the snare backbeat on beats two and four as a groove anchor throughout double bass passages — the kick and snare work together, not in opposition
- Shift instantly from upper-limb-led groove sections to leg-led double bass passages without rhythmic disruption — practice the transition point as its own drill
- Sustain double bass quality across full performance duration by maintaining economy of motion and heel-up technique without tightening the ankle under fatigue

### Key Elements

- Start at 120 BPM and focus entirely on foot evenness — record yourself and listen to whether the left and right kick attacks are identical in volume and timing
- Build to 162 BPM in 5 BPM increments across multiple sessions; speed gains consolidated slowly are more durable than those chased quickly
- Practice the ride cymbal and double bass independently first, then layer them together — the hands often lose their pattern quality when the feet first come in at full tempo
- Learn the verse-to-chorus double bass entry point as a specific drill: play 4 bars of the lighter verse groove, then drop into the full double bass on bar 5, and repeat until the entry is automatic and immediate

## Runes to My Memory Melodic Death Groove

**Song:** Runes to My Memory | **Album:** With Oden on Our Side (2006) — live staple of Wallgren's tenure | **BPM:** ~148 BPM | **Technique:** main groove | **Difficulty:** advanced

"Runes to My Memory" from Amon Amarth's 2006 album *With Oden on Our Side* is one of the band's most melodically rich tracks and a regular fixture in their live setlists — a song that requires the drummer to serve both the melodic sweep of the guitar work and the rhythmic power of the Viking metal aesthetic simultaneously. Jocke Wallgren, who joined Amon Amarth in 2016, has performed the song extensively across the band's global touring cycle, and his approach to its main groove — at approximately 148 BPM — demonstrates the groove-focused, anthemic style that characterises his contribution to the band's live sound. The song's main groove operates in a Gothenburg melodic death metal pocket that is distinct from the blast-beat intensity of pure death metal: the ride cymbal or hi-hat drives a steady eighth-note or sixteenth-note pulse that carries the song's melodic momentum, the snare hits with authority on beats two and four as a clear rhythmic anchor, and the kick pattern combines a driving downbeat figure with targeted double bass bursts that add density without overcomplicating the arrangement's melodic space. Wallgren's groove orientation is the defining characteristic of his performance: where a more technically oriented drummer might insert continuous double bass under the melodic riff passages, Wallgren selects his double bass placements to support the riff's rhythmic accent points specifically, creating a kick pattern that converses with the guitar's dynamics rather than competing with it. The pre-chorus sections introduce increasing double bass density that builds energy heading into the chorus, where Wallgren opens the pattern to a broader, anthem-supporting feel — the ride opens up, the snare carries more weight, and the overall arrangement gains the scale that matches Amon Amarth's typically large-stage presentation. The song also features bridge sections where the tempo and feel shift, demonstrating Wallgren's ability to transition between different rhythmic contexts within the same song without losing the overall momentum arc. His live performances of "Runes to My Memory" reveal a drummer who combines physical power with compositional awareness: he hits hard and consistently, but the choices about where to hit and where not to are deliberate and serve the song's narrative structure. For drummers, "Runes to My Memory" is an excellent entry point into melodic death metal groove playing: it develops the ride/hi-hat pulse technique that underpins the Gothenburg sound, the targeted double bass placement that serves melodic arrangements, the snare authority that anchors an anthemic arrangement, and the dynamic range to move from verse restraint to chorus power within a continuous performance.

### How to Play

- Drive the main groove with a steady eighth-note ride or hi-hat pulse that carries the melodic forward momentum without cluttering the guitar's harmonic space
- Place double bass bursts at the riff's rhythmic accent points specifically — select rather than blanket the groove with continuous kick density
- Increase double bass density in pre-chorus sections as a deliberate build toward the more expansive chorus arrangement
- Open the ride and increase snare authority for the chorus to match the song's anthemic scale shift from verse restraint to full power
- Navigate feel shifts in bridge sections cleanly — the overall momentum arc continues through these transitions even when the immediate rhythmic feel changes

### Key Elements

- Learn the ride pattern and snare backbeat without kick first — the upper-limb pulse is the foundation that the kick patterns build on top of
- Map the double bass placement points by listening to the guitar riff and identifying the specific accent notes where kick density would reinforce (not obscure) the melody
- Practice the pre-chorus build as a standalone four-to-eight bar phrase: start at the verse kick density, gradually increase double bass over the build bars, land the chorus with maximum authority
- Record the full groove and listen back to whether your verse feel has the restraint the section requires — many drummers overfill verse sections in melodic death metal out of habit from heavier material

## Fafner's Gold Blast Beat Section

**Song:** Fafner's Gold | **Album:** Berserker (2019) | **BPM:** ~160 BPM | **Technique:** blast beat | **Difficulty:** advanced

"Fafner's Gold" opens Amon Amarth's 2019 album *Berserker* — the first album track Jocke Wallgren recorded in a lead studio role with the band after joining in 2016 for *Jomsviking* — and it features the blast beat passages that stand as some of the most demanding drumming of Wallgren's recorded output. The song operates at approximately 160 BPM and introduces blast beat sections within its otherwise groove-oriented melodic death metal framework, creating a dynamic structure where the blast passages serve as intensification moments within a predominantly power-groove song rather than as the song's primary rhythmic mode. Wallgren's approach to blast beats within Amon Amarth's melodic context is stylistically distinct from the full-velocity blasting of pure death metal: his blast patterns in "Fafner's Gold" are melodic death metal blasts — alternating kick and snare at full speed while the hands drive the ride or hi-hat in a pattern that preserves the song's anthemic character, rather than the wall-of-percussion density of more extreme genres. The crucial technical detail in Wallgren's blast beat execution is the preservation of clarity between the snare and kick in each alternating stroke: at 160 BPM, both elements must have sufficient separation and weight to register distinctly rather than blurring into an undifferentiated mass, and his physical technique — a combination of tight snare stroke with full rebound and heel-up kick pedal engagement — provides this clarity even at sustained extreme tempo. The song's structure creates specific demands that pure blast beat practice does not prepare you for: the transitions from the groove-based sections into the blast passages must happen immediately at the correct tempo without a transition ramp, and the exit from the blast back into the groove must similarly be instant and in-pocket, requiring the drummer to maintain temporal awareness of both the blast pulse and the underlying song tempo simultaneously. Wallgren's blast beat passages are also notable for the anthemic quality he maintains even at maximum intensity: the snare cuts through with authoritative weight rather than reducing to a high-speed hi-hat-style ticking, a characteristic of his physically powerful striking approach. The *Berserker* production captures this authority well, and his performance on "Fafner's Gold" documents a drummer who has built genuine extreme-tempo capability within the specific context of melodic death metal's thematic and sonic requirements. For drummers, "Fafner's Gold" is the essential melodic death metal blast beat contextualisation study: it develops blast entry and exit precision in a groove song context, the striking authority needed to maintain snare clarity at 160 BPM, and the stylistic understanding of how blasting functions within melodic death metal differently from pure death or black metal.

### How to Play

- Execute alternating kick-snare blast at ~160 BPM with sufficient separation between each element that both register with distinct weight rather than blurring
- Drive the ride or hi-hat above the blast in a pattern that preserves the song's anthemic melodic-death-metal character — this is not a blackened wall-of-percussion blast
- Enter blast passages from groove sections at the correct tempo instantly with no acceleration ramp — maintain temporal awareness of the underlying song pulse throughout the groove sections leading into each blast
- Strike the snare with full physical authority even at blast tempo — the authoritative cut of a hard snare stroke distinguishes melodic death metal blasting from thinner, faster extreme metal variants
- Exit blast passages back into the groove feel cleanly and in-pocket — the re-entry must sit exactly on the beat without rushing or overshooting

### Key Elements

- Build the blast at 120 BPM and focus on snare authority rather than speed — a heavy, clear snare stroke at slow tempo builds the muscle pattern for a heavy, clear snare at 160 BPM
- Practice blast entry from a groove: play 8 bars of a groove at the song tempo, then enter the blast on bar 9 with no acceleration — the snap in is the hardest part
- Work on blast exit independently: run 8 bars of blast, then land a groove downbeat on bar 9 — the re-entry must feel immediate and in-pocket
- Record blast passages and count the stroke evenness: each kick should be the same volume and each snare the same volume — dynamic drift across the blast passage is the first sign of technique breakdown under fatigue
