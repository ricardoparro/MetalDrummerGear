## Epidemic of Hate Gravity Blast

**Song:** Epidemic of Hate | **Album:** Killing on Adrenaline (1998) | **BPM:** ~200 BPM | **Technique:** blast technique | **Difficulty:** expert

Kevin Talley's work on Dying Fetus's 1998 debut full-length Killing on Adrenaline introduced a wider extreme-metal audience to the gravity blast — a snare-driven technique in which the stick's free fall and natural rebound generate the stroke rather than active arm or wrist force. On "Epidemic of Hate" Talley deploys this approach in the drum intro and throughout the song's fastest sections, creating a wash of snare articulation that remains audible and even where a traditional alternating blast would collapse into noise. The gravity blast differs mechanically from a standard blast: the stick is held loosely at the butt end, angled so gravity pulls it into the head, and the rebound is allowed to carry it back — the player controls the angle and catch rather than driving the stroke. Because the energy comes from physics rather than muscular effort, a well-executed gravity blast can sustain tempos that would fatigue any limb-driven technique within seconds. What makes Talley's version so instructive is his consistency: the snare stays even and round across long passages, which means every aspect of his setup — grip, stick angle, head tension, and rebound control — is dialled in together. Learning this technique requires patience and a willingness to unlearn conventional grip habits, but the payoff is a blast that is both faster and more enduring than most drummers achieve with orthodox approaches. For death-metal drummers, understanding the gravity blast opens up a tier of speed that is otherwise physically inaccessible, and "Epidemic of Hate" is one of the most direct demonstrations of what that technique sounds like in a real song context.

### How to Play

- Hold the stick loosely at the butt end and angle it so gravity pulls it into the snare head
- Allow the rebound to carry the stick back rather than driving the return stroke with wrist or arm
- Lock the kick under the snare so the blast stays articulate even at extreme tempo
- Control stick angle and catch point rather than grip pressure to adjust speed
- Build endurance by sustaining long passages at a relaxed tempo before pushing the ceiling

### Key Elements

- Practise the gravity stroke with one hand only until the angle and rebound feel natural before adding the second hand
- Keep a very loose grip — any tension in the hand prevents the rebound from doing its job
- Start at a tempo where every stroke rebounds cleanly, not the fastest you can manage
- Film from the side to check stick angle: the stick should fall nearly parallel to the head surface, not perpendicular

## Killing on Adrenaline Two-Handed Blast

**Song:** Killing on Adrenaline | **Album:** Killing on Adrenaline (1998) | **BPM:** ~220 BPM | **Technique:** signature pattern | **Difficulty:** expert

The title track of Dying Fetus's 1998 debut is a relentless showcase of Kevin Talley's two-handed blast approach — a technique where both hands strike simultaneously or in very tight alternation on the snare while the double bass drives underneath, creating a dense, wall-of-sound blast character distinct from the open, rebounding quality of a gravity blast. At around 220 BPM Talley maintains absolute evenness across both hands, which requires that the matching grip, stick height, and wrist mechanics are genuinely symmetrical — any imbalance between the dominant and non-dominant hand becomes audible instantly at this tempo. The two-handed blast is physically demanding in a different way from the gravity blast: rather than relying on physics to carry the stroke, the player must generate and control speed through muscular endurance, which means conditioning matters as much as technique. What sets Talley apart in this context is the integration of his double bass: the feet move as a continuous engine under the hands without any loss of rhythmic lockup, so the blast never sounds like two independent events bolted together but like a single coordinated machine. For drummers, studying Talley's two-handed blast builds left-hand and non-dominant-foot independence, since both the weaker hand and the left foot must perform at the same level as their dominant counterparts for the pattern to hold together. The title track of Killing on Adrenaline is one of the most direct templates for grindcore-adjacent death-metal blasting in the Maryland death metal tradition that Dying Fetus helped define.

### How to Play

- Strike the snare with both hands in matched alternation, keeping wrist mechanics identical between dominant and non-dominant hands
- Drive the double bass as a continuous engine under the snare so the blast sounds unified rather than layered
- Keep stick height equally low on both sides so neither hand lifts and slows the pattern
- Develop the weaker hand in isolation before combining it with the strong hand and feet
- Build conditioning with timed blast reps at a sustainable tempo to grow endurance alongside speed

### Key Elements

- Practise the non-dominant hand alone with a metronome until it matches the dominant hand exactly in tone and consistency
- Add the double bass only after both hands are even — the feet amplify any existing imbalance
- Use a timer to build blast endurance in sets: 30 seconds on, 30 seconds rest, gradually extending the work intervals
- Record audio as well as video to hear whether both hands are producing the same volume and articulation

## Destroy the Opposition Groove Breakdown

**Song:** Destroy the Opposition | **Album:** Destroy the Opposition (2000) | **BPM:** ~155 BPM | **Technique:** main groove | **Difficulty:** advanced

One of Kevin Talley's most underappreciated qualities is his ability to shift gears between extreme blasting and punishing mid-tempo groove, and the title track of Dying Fetus's 2000 album Destroy the Opposition is a prime example of that duality. The groove sections on this record sit around 155 BPM but hit with a physical weight that can feel heavier than many faster passages, because Talley places the snare and kick with surgical precision rather than filling space with density. This approach to death-metal groove — sometimes called a "chug" or "breakdown" pattern — requires the drummer to let notes breathe, which is a different discipline entirely from blasting: instead of sustaining speed, the challenge is resisting the temptation to fill gaps and trusting that the space between notes is doing work. What gives Talley's groove playing its authority is the consistency of his dynamics: the snare hits at a uniform volume so the pocket stays deep, and the kick locks in with the bass guitar rather than drifting independently, giving Dying Fetus their signature tight low-end chug. For drummers, this approach is a useful reminder that death metal technique is not only about maximum speed — controlling a groove at 155 BPM with consistent dynamics and a locked pocket is its own demanding skill. The video in this study shows Talley demonstrating his full technical range, giving an excellent frame for understanding how his groove vocabulary connects to his blasting approach.

### How to Play

- Place the snare squarely in the pocket and let notes breathe rather than filling every gap
- Lock the kick with the bass guitar so the low-end chug stays tight rather than independent
- Keep snare dynamics uniform hit-to-hit so the groove stays deep rather than wandering in volume
- Use linear patterns where kick and snare rarely overlap to create clarity in the low-mid range
- Practise the groove alongside a bass guitar recording to feel when the pocket locks and when it drifts

### Key Elements

- Slow the groove to 80 BPM and listen for any inconsistency in snare volume before bringing tempo up
- Record with a bass guitar backing track to check whether the kick genuinely locks with the bass line
- Resist filling every gap — the discipline of leaving space is the point of this style
- Focus on dynamics: if the snare volume varies hit-to-hit, the pocket will feel unsteady to the rest of the band
