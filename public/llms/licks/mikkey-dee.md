## Ace of Spades Driving Power

**Song:** Ace of Spades | **Album:** Ace of Spades (2008 re-recording) | **BPM:** ~280 BPM | **Technique:** signature pattern | **Difficulty:** advanced

"Ace of Spades" is the song that defines Motörhead, and although Phil Taylor played on the 1980 original, Mikkey Dee — Motörhead's drummer from 1992 until Lemmy's passing in 2015 — re-recorded it in 2008 and powered it on stage night after night for over two decades. Mikkey's version is a clinic in relentless, high-octane rock-metal drumming: the tempo is brutal, but he drives it with a punishing, locked-in groove, heavy backbeat, and the explosive energy that made Motörhead a force of nature. His signature is raw power married to rock-solid time — he plays fast and hard without ever rushing, and his fills are aggressive bursts that propel the song rather than decorate it. Mikkey is also a celebrated double-bass and technical player, but on "Ace of Spades" he channels all of that ability into pure drive, proving that taste and intensity matter as much as chops. In the isolated drum track you can hear how disciplined the part is beneath the chaos: the groove never wavers, the backbeat is enormous, and every fill lands clean. For drummers, "Ace of Spades" is a study in high-tempo rock-metal stamina and feel: it develops fast, steady time, a powerful backbeat that holds up at speed, and the endurance to sustain full intensity for a whole song. The approach is to lock the groove to a metronome and build the tempo gradually, keep the body relaxed so the speed is sustainable, and play the fills as controlled bursts. It's a definitive example of how Mikkey Dee turned breakneck speed into an unstoppable groove.

- Lock a punishing, steady groove at breakneck tempo without rushing
- Anchor an enormous backbeat that holds up at speed
- Drive the song with aggressive fill bursts, not decoration

- Build the tempo gradually and stay relaxed for stamina

**Song:** Overkill | **Album:** Overkill (2008 re-recording) | **BPM:** ~195 BPM | **Technique:** signature pattern | **Difficulty:** advanced

"Overkill" is famous for one of the most influential drum parts in heavy metal — the relentless double-bass pattern that rumbles like a freight train and is widely credited as a foundational moment for double-bass drumming in the genre. Phil Taylor created it on the 1979 original, but Mikkey Dee re-recorded the song in 2008 and made its thundering double bass a highlight of Motörhead's live shows, including the famous false endings where the band stops and the drums roar back to life. Mikkey's interpretation is a powerhouse: he drives the iconic double-bass roll with even, controlled feet and a huge backbeat, then unleashes the dramatic stop-start dynamics that make "Overkill" so thrilling live. His signature here is the combination of stamina and showmanship — sustaining that pounding double bass while milking the song's theatrical structure. In the isolated drum track you can hear how steady the double bass stays under the chaos and how precisely he hits the song's dynamic shifts. For drummers, "Overkill" is a classic study in double-bass drumming: it develops even, sustained double-bass technique, the endurance to keep it going, and the control to play dramatic stop-start dynamics cleanly. The approach is to build the double-bass roll slowly until both feet are even, develop the stamina to sustain it, and practise the false-ending hits so they land tight. It's a cornerstone of metal drumming history and a perfect vehicle for Mikkey Dee's power.

## Bastards Motorik Drive

**Song:** Death or Glory | **Album:** Bastards (1993) | **BPM:** ~138 BPM | **Technique:** main groove | **Difficulty:** advanced

When Mikkey Dee joined Motörhead in 1992, he brought a technical pedigree that previous Motörhead drummers had not possessed — he could play the motorik locomotive groove that defined the band's sound at any tempo, while also adding double-bass runs and dynamism that pushed the band's live performances to new heights. The Bastards album (1993), recorded at the Abyss Studio in Sweden, was his first full studio recording with the band, and it captures exactly that blend: the relentless motorik 8th-note drive that is Motörhead's rhythmic signature, now executed with the power and precision of a player at the top of his craft. "Death or Glory" epitomises this approach — a driving, forward-moving rocker built on the locomotive groove that made Motörhead a force of nature. The motorik groove at the heart of Motörhead's sound is deceptively disciplined. The 8th-note hi-hat (or open hi-hat with a slight groove wash) runs as an unwavering engine beneath the whole arrangement, the snare hits hard and consistently on the two and four, and the kick anchors the downbeat and occasional syncopations without overreaching. What makes this kind of playing difficult is not technical complexity but physical consistency: holding that locked, driving 8th-note feel at high tempos for entire songs requires that the swing be constant, the backbeat authoritative, and the hi-hat even without becoming mechanical. Mikkey Dee's genius on Bastards is that the groove always feels alive — there is weight and personality in every stroke — even as it locks with metronomic precision. For the Bastards sessions Dee used a Sonor kit, which contributed to the album's dense, powerful drum sound: the shells are large, the tuning is relatively low, and the snare has a thick, punchy crack rather than a high-pitched snap. Learning to internalise that specific weight of sound is part of understanding the motorik groove — it is not just a rhythmic feel but a sonic commitment. For drummers, this lick develops endurance at tempo, hi-hat consistency, and the discipline to serve a groove rather than embellish it. Practise the 8th-note hi-hat at a variety of tempos with a metronome, locking the backbeat and kick to it without tension, then push the tempo until the groove feels effortless. That relentless, forward drive is what Motörhead was, and Mikkey Dee's Bastards playing is its purest expression on record.

### How to Play

- Drive the motorik 8th-note hi-hat as an unwavering locomotive engine throughout
- Anchor a powerful, consistent backbeat on two and four without rushing
- Lock the kick to the downbeat and keep syncopations deliberate and in-the-pocket
- Maintain personality and weight in every stroke even as the groove locks metronomically
- Build hi-hat endurance at tempo before adding kick and snare

### Key Elements

- Practice the 8th-note hi-hat alone with a metronome at a range of tempos until it feels natural
- Add the backbeat next — keep it heavy and consistent before introducing the kick
- Stay relaxed in the grip; tension at speed kills the locomotive feel
- Listen to Bastards closely and copy the weight and attitude of each stroke

## Bomber Triplet Gallop

**Song:** Bomber | **Album:** Bomber (1979, live with Mikkey Dee) | **BPM:** ~150 BPM | **Technique:** signature pattern | **Difficulty:** advanced

"Bomber" is one of Motörhead's most loved stage anthems — a thunderous, mid-fast rocker from the 1979 Bomber album that became a permanent fixture of their live show, complete with the famous aircraft rig that descended over the audience at the climax. Phil Taylor's original recording drove the song with a punishing groove, but Mikkey Dee — who played "Bomber" live for over two decades — gave the gallop pattern his own stamp: heavier, more controlled, and technically cleaner than the raw energy of the original, without losing an ounce of the song's menace. The gallop is a rhythmic pattern built on triplet subdivisions: rather than a straight quarter-note feel, the kick and hi-hat play a loping, three-against-two pulse that gives "Bomber" its driving, slightly lopsided momentum. On Mikkey's version, this gallop is executed with real precision — the triplets are even, the kick is articulate, and the snare sits in exactly the right place relative to the guitar riff to lock the whole arrangement together. The challenge is maintaining the evenness of the triplet subdivisions at speed without letting the gallop rush or drag, which requires internalising the feel so thoroughly that it runs on autopilot while the hands and feet play their independent roles. What makes the "Bomber" gallop such a rewarding pattern to learn is that it appears in countless heavy rock and metal songs — once you have it internalised, you recognise it everywhere and can apply it across styles. It also develops your sense of triplet subdivision, which is foundational for swing feels, shuffle grooves, and any playing that sits between straight 8ths and a full 12/8. For drummers, learning it in the context of Mikkey Dee's playing means adding the combination of power and control that defines Motörhead's sound: the pattern should not feel academic, it should feel like a vehicle accelerating toward the horizon. Practise the gallop at a slow tempo until the subdivisions feel natural and even, then add speed in small increments, keeping the snare and kick articulate throughout. Apply Dee's signature heavy backbeat on top of the gallop feel and the result is one of the most useful grooves in all of heavy rock drumming.

### How to Play

- Build the triplet gallop feel by counting three-against-two subdivisions aloud first
- Keep the kick articulate and even within the triplet grouping — avoid smearing the pulse
- Anchor a heavy, decisive backbeat on two and four over the gallop subdivision
- Maintain even triplet subdivision at speed through relaxed grip and efficient stick return
- Practise the gallop at slow tempo before adding the Motörhead power and intensity

### Key Elements

- Count the triplet gallop aloud before touching the kit — feel the three-against-two first
- Slow the tempo dramatically and build evenness before adding any speed
- Apply the Motörhead intensity only once the subdivisions are completely natural
- Listen to live recordings of Dee playing Bomber to absorb the weight and feel

## Overkill 8th-Note Double Bass

**Song:** Overkill | **Album:** Overkill (1979, performed by Mikkey Dee) | **BPM:** ~195 BPM | **Technique:** double bass | **Difficulty:** advanced

The double-bass pattern on "Overkill" is one of the most influential drum parts in heavy metal history — a relentless, churning roll of 8th notes that rumbles beneath the song like an engine with no governor. Phil Taylor's 1979 original created the template, but Mikkey Dee's live performances of the song made the double-bass the focal point of his tenure with Motörhead: he brought the pattern his exceptional foot technique, his ability to sustain even, articulate kick strokes at high velocity, and his showmanship in milking the song's dramatic stop-start dynamics for maximum crowd impact. The specific technique Dee applies to the Overkill double bass is worth examining closely, because it differs from the blast-beat-derived double bass that drives extreme metal. Rather than a burst of maximum speed, the Overkill pattern is a controlled, even roll of 8th-note kick strokes — both feet alternating in perfect subdivision, with neither foot dominating or dragging. At 195 BPM, this means each foot is playing at approximately 97.5 BPM individually, which is demanding but sustainable with proper heel-up technique and ankle drive. The key is that every stroke sounds identical in volume, attack, and decay — the bass drum pattern should sound like a single foot playing at speed, not one dominant foot and a weaker partner. Mikkey's years of practice and live performance have made this evenness completely natural, and it is the thing that separates his version of the pattern from a merely competent execution. The dramatic false endings and stop-start dynamics that make "Overkill" thrilling live depend entirely on this foot precision: when the band drops out and then the drums roar back in, the double bass has to restart at full intensity immediately without any warm-up. For drummers working on double-bass technique, "Overkill" is a perfect vehicle because the pattern is simple enough to focus entirely on evenness and stamina without navigating complex coordination challenges. Start with a four-bar 8th-note roll at 80 BPM, focus on making both feet sound absolutely identical, then extend the duration before increasing tempo. Build in 5 BPM increments, only advancing once both feet remain matched at the current speed for a full minute without fatigue. Practising this way builds the ankle control and muscle memory that make long double-bass passages sustainable rather than survivable.

### How to Play

- Alternate feet in even 8th-note strokes — every stroke identical in volume, attack, and decay
- Use heel-up technique with ankle drive rather than leg force to sustain the roll
- Keep both feet perfectly matched so the pattern sounds like a single foot at speed
- Practise the false-ending restarts so the double bass fires immediately at full intensity
- Build duration at a comfortable tempo before increasing speed

### Key Elements

- Practise the 8th-note roll at 80 BPM in 4-bar loops until both feet are identical
- Extend the duration of each rep before increasing tempo
- Listen critically for the weaker foot and work to match it to the stronger
- Build 5 BPM at a time — only advance when the current tempo holds for a full minute
