## Sprite Metric Modulation Study

**Song:** Sprite | **Album:** Empath (2019) | **BPM:** shifting (~130–165 BPM) | **Technique:** signature pattern | **Difficulty:** expert

Devin Townsend's 2019 album Empath is one of the most ambitious progressive metal records in recent memory, and Morgan Ågren's drumming across it is a masterclass in applying metric modulation to a rock context. On "Sprite" in particular, Ågren navigates a series of time-signature shifts — moving between 4/4, 7/8, and 5/4 passages — without ever losing the underlying pulse that makes the song feel coherent rather than chaotic. Metric modulation, in the sense Ågren uses it here, means that the felt tempo changes but the subdivision remains constant: a quarter note in 4/4 becomes a dotted quarter in the new time signature, so the beat shifts but the listener's internal clock does not lurch. This is a technique rooted in contemporary classical composition and jazz, and Ågren's ability to apply it in a progressive metal arrangement reflects a career-long engagement with rhythmically sophisticated music through his work with the Mats/Morgan Band and his early collaboration with Frank Zappa's circle. The playthrough video he released for "Sprite" provides a rare clear view of how he physically navigates the shifts — the hi-hat foot and ride hand function as anchor points that carry the subdivision across the bar-line change while the snare resets to the new metric feel. For drummers, learning to play through metric modulations requires internalising the subdivision rather than counting bar by bar, which is a fundamental shift in how to relate to pulse. Ågren's playing on Empath is an ideal case study because the modulations are compositionally motivated — they serve the music rather than being displayed for their own sake — which means every shift feels earned rather than arbitrary.

### How to Play

- Keep the subdivision constant across bar-line changes so the pulse feels continuous rather than lurching
- Use the hi-hat foot as an anchor subdivision marker that persists through time-signature shifts
- Internalise the new metric feel before the downbeat of each new section rather than recounting from bar one
- Practise each time signature in isolation until it feels natural, then practise the transition points specifically
- Sing or tap the melodic line while playing to keep the modulations serving the music rather than dominating it

### Key Elements

- Record the modulation transitions and listen back critically — the subdivision should feel seamless, not like a reset
- Practise with a click set to the common subdivision, not the beat, so the click persists across time-signature changes
- Tap the melodic line while practising the drum part to understand where the modulation points align with the composition
- Work through time signatures one relationship at a time (4/4 → 7/8, then 7/8 → 5/4) rather than the full sequence at once

## Odd-Meter Improvisation Study

**Song:** Drum Set Improvisation | **Album:** ArtOfDrumming Clinic (2021) | **BPM:** ~120 BPM (shifting meters) | **Technique:** signature pattern | **Difficulty:** expert

Morgan Ågren's approach to odd-meter improvisation is one of the most instructive examples in contemporary drumming of how to make unconventional time signatures feel musical rather than academic. In this ArtOfDrumming clinic performance, Ågren improvises through a series of odd meters — 7/8, 9/8, and 11/8 among them — treating them not as puzzles to be solved but as rhythmic characters with their own feel and momentum. This is a fundamentally different relationship to odd time than the counting-based approach most drummers learn first: rather than parsing 7/8 as 3+4 or 4+3 and counting through it, Ågren feels the group as a single rhythmic unit that has its own internal weight distribution. This capacity comes from decades of playing with pianist Mats Öberg in the Mats/Morgan Band, where odd meters are the compositional norm rather than the exception, and from the influence of Frank Zappa's rigorously metered compositional style, which Ågren engaged with directly during the Zappa Universe projects in the 1990s. What the clinic performance reveals is how Ågren maintains a conversation with the groove even inside the odd groupings: the hi-hats and ride keep a musical statement going rather than just marking the meter, and fills resolve in ways that feel inevitable rather than arbitrary. For drummers, the exercise is to learn each odd grouping as a bodily feeling — a 7 has a different physical weight from an 8 or a 9 — and then practise switching between them while keeping the music moving. Ågren's improvisations make this look effortless, which is the result of years of making odd meters the default rather than the exception.

### How to Play

- Feel each odd grouping as a bodily unit rather than counting subdivisions — a 7/8 should have a single internal weight distribution
- Keep the hi-hat or ride making a musical statement rather than just marking the meter through odd bars
- Practise moving between 7/8, 9/8, and 11/8 with a constant eighth-note reference so the transitions feel continuous
- Resolve fills to the downbeat of the new grouping rather than the old one to make the meter change feel inevitable
- Play odd-meter patterns daily until they feel as natural as 4/4 — fluency comes from habituation, not calculation

### Key Elements

- Practise walking and clapping a 7/8 pattern before playing it on the kit — embodied feeling precedes technical execution
- Set a metronome to the eighth-note subdivision and practise switching meters over it so the pulse never breaks
- Compose short one- or two-bar odd-meter ostinatos and improvise fills over them rather than free-improvising from scratch
- Listen extensively to the Mats/Morgan Band catalogue — passive listening in odd meters accelerates internalisation

## Neyveli Prog Groove Performance

**Song:** Neyveli | **Album:** ArtOfDrumming Feature (2021) | **BPM:** ~160 BPM | **Technique:** main groove | **Difficulty:** advanced

The ArtOfDrumming Neyveli performance feature captures Morgan Ågren applying his full rhythmic vocabulary to a focused groove context, and it is one of the clearest illustrations of what separates a prog-inflected groove from a straightforward rock beat. Ågren plays in 4/4 but uses metric displacement — phrasing rhythmic motifs that start in unexpected places in the bar — to give the groove a floating, shifting quality while the underlying pulse remains absolutely solid. This is a device borrowed from jazz and contemporary classical composition, but Ågren deploys it in a way that feels immediately physical and musical rather than theoretical. The groove never loses its forward motion even when individual phrases are displaced by a dotted quarter or a half-beat, which is the mark of a drummer who has internalised the technique to the point where the displacement is expressive rather than disorienting. What makes Ågren's groove playing so valuable to study is that it demonstrates a principle applicable to any style: a locked, confident pulse is not the same thing as a metrically plain one. You can be completely in time while playing phrases that begin off the anticipated beat, and the contrast between the solid pulse and the displaced phrase is precisely what creates rhythmic tension and interest. For drummers, developing this skill requires separating groove consistency — the bass drum and hi-hat locking the pulse — from phrase freedom, where the snare and secondary percussion can start anywhere in the bar. Ågren's Neyveli performance is a graduate-level lesson in exactly that independence.

### How to Play

- Keep the bass drum and hi-hat absolutely locked to the pulse even while snare phrases start on displaced beats
- Treat metric displacement as an expressive tool — begin rhythmic motifs a dotted quarter or half-beat early or late for tension
- Maintain consistent groove dynamics so the displaced phrases land with authority rather than feeling hesitant
- Practise the two-layer approach: first lock the pulse limbs alone, then improvise displaced phrases over them without disturbing the foundation
- Listen back to recordings and ask whether the displaced phrases feel intentional and musical or merely accidental

### Key Elements

- Practise locking the bass drum and hi-hat to a click while freeing the snare to land on any subdivision — the two layers should not influence each other
- Start displacement exercises by moving snare hits one eighth-note late or early before attempting dotted-quarter displacements
- Record and listen critically: if the pulse feels unstable when phrases displace, strengthen the pulse foundation before adding complexity
- Transcribe Ågren's phrase start-points in the Neyveli performance and work them into your own groove practice as a vocabulary exercise
